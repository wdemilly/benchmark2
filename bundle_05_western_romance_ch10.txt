STEP 2A — ASSESS THREE DRAFTS AND SELECT ONE
(v1 — 2026-07-18. New step. Purpose: the same chapter is drafted three times by Step 2; this step reads all three as literature and selects the one to keep. This is a craft judgment, not a measurement. Derived from a close comparison of three Keystone chapter 2 drafts whose quality differences were large and identifiable; the criteria below are the differences that mattered there. To be run in a fresh incognito window with no other context. Amended 21 July 2026 on Walter's instruction: criteria 3 and 4 are swapped, so dialogue under load is now judged third and information strategy fourth. Reason: on Keystone chapter 10b the old order selected the draft that scored 84 over the draft that scored 91, and the assessment itself named the cause — it ranked the winning draft best on dialogue and then said the criterion order broke the tie against it. Under the new order it selects the 91.)

You are a fiction editor reading three drafts of the same chapter. All three were written from the same outline. Your job is to judge them as prose fiction — as an acquiring editor would — and select the strongest. You are not checking them against the outline for coverage; assume all three cover it.

Read all three drafts completely before writing anything.

Then assess each draft against these criteria, in this order. For every judgment you make, quote the exact passage it rests on. A judgment without a quotation does not count.

1. INTERIORITY. When the chapter goes inside the point-of-view character, is the interior material pinned to specific remembered places, objects, and people, or does it run to summary and abstraction? A memory with a name and a physical detail outranks a reflection that tells the reader what to conclude. Quote the best-pinned interior moment and the most abstract one in each draft.

2. THEME HANDLING. Does any character state the chapter's theme out loud? A speech that announces the meaning of the story is a defect, however well written. Behavior that carries the meaning without naming it is the standard. Quote the most thesis-like line in each draft and the best moment where meaning is carried by action alone.

3. DIALOGUE UNDER LOAD. Find the exchange that carries the most story weight in each draft. Does the dialogue do more than one job at a time — advance the plot, reveal the speaker, and obstruct the other character in the same lines? Quote the single best line of dialogue in each draft and say what jobs it is doing.

4. INFORMATION STRATEGY. What does each draft withhold, and how? The strongest choice lets the reader catch something a character misses, staged in plain sight without narration pointing at it. The weakest explains its own secrets. Identify each draft's central withheld fact and quote how it is planted.

5. MOTIF DISCIPLINE. Does a physical gesture or object recur with accumulated meaning — the same action returning later as metaphor, earned rather than installed? Quote the recurrence if it exists.

6. PRECISION OF THE PHYSICAL WORLD. Are measurements, procedures, and objects exact, consistent, and professionally credible throughout? Note any place where the physical facts blur, contradict, or go generic.

7. LINE FAULTS. Note clumsy constructions, repeated sentence shapes, tense wobbles, and dead phrases, with quotations. Weigh these last; a draft with the best architecture and a few rough lines beats a clean draft with weak architecture.

Then write the verdict:

- Rank the three drafts, first to third.
- For the winner, state in one paragraph why it wins, resting the paragraph on the quoted evidence above.
- For each loser, state in one or two sentences the decisive weakness.
- If two drafts are close, say so and say what would break the tie.
- Name the winner on its own final line, exactly: SELECTED: DRAFT [number].

Do not summarize the plot. Do not suggest revisions. Do not comment on the outline. Judge what is on the page.

----- DRAFT 1 -----

"The children want their teacher to see the foal," Cora Jessup said. Lettie had the week's slates in the crook of her arm. She set them on the hall table, stacked wrong side up, and pulled the door wider. Cora stood on the step with her hands down at her sides. Her dress had been patched at the right shoulder in a blue that never weathered to match the rest. "When did it come?" Lettie asked. "Wednesday night. Burr sat up with the mare till three." Cora stayed where she was. "They've asked me every day since. I told them a teacher's Saturday belongs to her." "It does." "Seven miles out and seven back," Cora said. "I'd think no less of you for saying no." "I'll come." The words went out ahead of the part of Lettie that usually ruled on them. In the dining room behind her, Mrs. Kreig poured coffee into a cup and took her time about it. "Noon dinner," Cora said. "It's beans, and it's Saturday, so there's cake." "Then I'll be there before noon." The wagon came up the street while Cora was still on the step, four bales roped high behind the seat. Nels set the brake and swung down and took the rope end in his fist. "Morning, Mrs. Jessup." He put a knee against the wagon side, hauled the knot down snug, and threw his weight into the load to feel it push back. "That's my second cutting. Burr can argue with me about it in June." "He will," Cora said. "Road runs past your gate." Nels worked the rope's tail under the last turn. "If Miss Pardee's going out, I can carry her and the walk stays home." Lettie had rules about wagons, and about who stood in a street watching her get up into one. She looked at the hay, and at Cora's shoulder, and heard the coffee pot go down on the sideboard behind her. "That would be a kindness," she said. "I'll want ten minutes." "Take them. The team's had a week of standing and they're not tired of it." Mrs. Kreig came through the hall, gathered the slates off the table, and went back to her kitchen without a word about any of it. Lettie came out with her satchel and climbed up by the hub and the seat rail. Nels loosed the brake, took the lines in both hands, and turned the team out of the yard. The wagon's creak came up under her and settled into a rhythm. Roan Creek went past on both sides, the mercantile with its awning down, Doss's stable, the church with its new step. Dust rolled off the wheels in a low sheet. Somewhere past the last fence she let go of the seat rail and put her hands in her lap. The road climbed out of the bottom and ran south and west into the foothills. Lupin stood thick and blue in the meadow grass on both sides of the ruts, and the sage had held the cold of the night in it and gave it back as the sun came onto the slopes. Snow lines still lay along the high ridges of the Bighorns. "You've not been out this far," Nels said. "To the Neeley place, in March. It was mud to the axle and I saw nothing at all." "March is mud. This is the two weeks the country charges you the rest of the year for." He shifted the lines to one hand. "Hold these a minute." Lettie took them. The leather was warm and thicker than she expected, and her palms sat on it plainly, the schoolteacher's hands the town had been remarking on since October. "They know the road," Nels said. "Keep them slack and let them think it was their idea." "That is my entire method with the third form." "How's it working?" "Sam Jessup read forty pages this week." "Is forty a lot?" "For Sam it is a career." She let the lines run through her fingers a little. "I put Cooper in front of him. I was down to trying anything." "Cooper's a swindle. Nobody in the world ever talked that way." "He's a swindle who got a boy through forty pages, so he and I are square." Nels laughed once, low, and took the lines back to swing the team wide of a washout. The near horse threw its head and settled. "What else does a Saturday teacher have to report." "The Hoyt girl sat down with the Jessups and the Krohn children on Thursday and shared out her dinner. Cold chicken. She gave the wing away without being asked." "Her father would want to sit down for that news." "He would want a chair with arms on it." "He'd want the chair and a doctor standing by." Nels checked the team down to a walk for a stretch of stones. "Hoyt's grass runs from that draw to the creek, all of it, and he's held it eleven years by looking at people. I'd not have bet on the chicken." "I take my victories in the size they come." The road bent and the foothills opened out, meadow running up to a bench of rock with the light standing full on it. Nels looked at it while he drove. "First fall I was up here I got off the horse on that bench and watched the shadow come across the whole of it," he said. "Took an hour. I had a horse that hadn't been fed and I let him wait." "Was he forgiving?" "He was not." "What's it worth, land like that?" Lettie asked. "I hear men in Kreig's dining room say numbers at each other every night and I've never once known whether they were serious." "Depends who's asking and what week." He nodded up at the ridge. "Grass like that with water on it, a man could run four hundred head and die of old age. Without the water he can run his mouth." "Then the water is the whole of it." "The water's the whole of everything out here. You'll hear a man talk for an hour about soil and mean a creek." Lettie unbuckled her satchel on her knees and buckled it again while she told him about the reader that came apart in her hands in February, and the stove that had to be coaxed with the door held at an angle, and what a room of thirty children smells like in a wet week. He asked what she did about the smell. She said she opened the windows and taught them geography of the Arctic. She had said three things without weighing them first, and she noticed on the fourth. The near wheel went into a rut and dropped hard. The board seat came up under her, and her hand shot out and caught his forearm, the muscle set hard and round under the sleeve, the heat of him through the cloth of it. She let go at once. "The county calls this a road," Nels said. "The county is generous." She put the hand back in her lap and left it there. Her fingers kept the shape they had closed in. Sage went by on the left, then a stretch of lupin, then the wheel tracks of somebody's mower. They rode the next mile with the wheels making the only sound in it. The quiet had a different weight in it than the quiet before. They came over the rise above the Jessup place at half past eleven. The house sat low with its chimney smoking, the corral beyond it, the mare and the foal in the near corner. Where the barn had been, four charred timbers stood up out of blackened ground. The ground had gone slick and grey with the rain since. A lean-to of raw new lumber leaned against nothing at the near end of the burn, and even at that distance the wind coming up the slope carried char. Nels backed the wagon to the lean-to. Lettie climbed down and crossed the black ground and put her hand flat against one of the standing posts. The surface gave under her palm, soft and crumbling to the depth of a knuckle, and when she took the hand away it was marked across the base of the fingers. She rubbed her thumb through the mark. Then she paced off the distance between the near posts and the far ones, twelve steps and twenty, and stood at the corner of it where a foundation stone still sat squared into the ground. The lean-to would take the wagon's four bales and about that much again. It had no door and no floor and one wall. The children came around the house at a run, Sam first, Ada holding the smallest one by the wrist. "Miss Pardee. His name's Ranger." "His name is not Ranger. We voted." "There was two of us voting and I won." "That," Lettie said, "is how it is done in this territory." Sam had the lead rope. The foal's halter had been let out to its last hole and still hung slack under his jaw, and he went sideways down the yard with all four legs in disagreement. Ada got the lead away from Sam by a method Lettie decided not to see, and the smallest one announced that the foal was hers on account of she had seen him first, which nobody troubled to answer. Behind them Nels dropped the tailgate and started the bales down into the lean-to, and Sam left the argument and went to take an end. They were quiet children in a yard, all of them. Ada picked the small one up when she wandered toward the burn and carried her the long way around to the paddock, and none of the three turned their heads to look at what they were walking past. Lettie walked them down to the rail and asked about the mare's feed and let Sam answer at length, and she kept her schoolroom face on the whole while, the one that had never yet been wrong in front of a child. Cora came out on the step with her hands in her apron. "They'll have him till dinner," she said. "Come in out of that wind." The kitchen was dark after the yard, and warm, and there was bread turned out on a cloth at the end of the table. Cora set a cup in front of Lettie and filled it and went back to the loaf, working the crust free with her thumbs. "You'll have seen the barn." "Yes." "The ninth of April. Four of them, and they came to the house first." Cora turned the loaf and set it down. "One of them stood about where you're sitting. He told Burr the offer was open till the twentieth and after the twentieth it wouldn't be an offer. He said it kindly. That's the part I keep." Lettie put both hands around the cup and left them there. "They had no covering over their faces. Burr knew one of them from the sale barn last fall." Cora took down a second cup. "He said no with his hand on that door and the children on the stairs behind him listening to him say it. Then they went out. The barn went at two." "You got the mare." "We got the mare and the two mules. We lost the hay, the harness, and Burr's father's plow, which he'd have carried out on his back if I'd let him past me." Cora filled the second cup and did not drink from it. "Ada stood at that window the whole of it with the little one on her hip. She was five weeks turned nine." "Did anyone come out from town?" "Dunphy rode out on the eleventh and looked at the ground and wrote in a book. He said a barn will go up from wet hay if it's stacked green." Cora set her palms flat on the table for a moment, then took them off it and went back to the loaf. "Burr stacked that hay in September and it was dry as paper, and I told Dunphy so, and he wrote that in the book too." "And since?" "A man rode past on the Tuesday. He didn't stop. He looked at the lean-to going up and he rode on, and he tipped his hat to me." Cora's thumbs went along the crust. "He won't sell. It's his name on the entry and he proved up on it and he'd sooner sit in the ash of it. I'd sell tonight. I'd sell tonight and take the children to my sister in Cheyenne and never say the name of this county again, and I can't, because he's my husband and he's right." "How long does the hay hold you?" "August, with what's come in today." Cora smoothed the cloth over the bread. "I sleep three hours. I get up at any noise and I stand at that window and I count the children twice, and I've stopped pretending to Burr that I don't. I am afraid all the time now. I don't know how much longer afraid is a thing I can manage." Lettie's face stayed at the setting she used when a child came in bleeding and the room had to be held. She reached across the table and took Cora's hand off the cloth and held it. Cora's fingers were floury and hot from the oven. Lettie did not let go when the moment for letting go went by, and she did not let go after that. Out in the yard the tailgate went up with a bang, and the last of the bales were in. Burr Jessup stood at the edge of the lean-to with his hat in his hand while Nels forked the top course back into the corner. He came in for the last two and carried them himself. "I'll settle with you," Burr said. "You'll settle with me in June, and I'll charge you for the hauling." "You'll charge me nothing for the hauling." "Then June," Nels said, and coiled the rope and dropped it into the empty bed. Lettie went down the yard to the paddock, where Ada had the foal's lead wound twice around her fist. "Monday," Lettie said, and her voice came out an inch off from ordinary, and she brought it back. "Sam. Forty more pages, and I want to know whether the man ever gets across the water." "He gets across," Sam said. "I read ahead." "Then don't tell me." She got up on the wheel and onto the seat, and Ada lifted the small one's arm and waved it for her. The wagon was loud going home, empty over the ruts, everything in it ringing that had been quiet under the load. The sun went down behind the near hills and left the Bighorns standing on the right with the last of the light coming off their ridges. Lettie worked the satchel buckle open and shut on her knees. "Hay holds them to August," Nels said. "That's what she told me." "After that there's his own grass, if the summer's fair to him." "And if it isn't." "Then it isn't." He put the brake on with his boot for the long downgrade and let the team walk it. "Road's better on this side. You'll not need to grab anything." "I'll try to contain myself." She braced her boot flat against the footboard and rode the grade with the brake groaning under them. The buckle stayed shut under her hand. He drove, and the Bighorns went from grey to nothing on the right, and she stopped filling the space between them and found she was not required to. The boarding house had a lamp in the front window when he brought the team up. Nels set the brake and came around and stood while she got herself down by the hub. "Monday," Lettie said. "Monday." He drove off down the street. Lettie went up the steps and through the hall. At the foot of the stairs she looked once toward the top of them, where her room was with the trunk under the bed in it. Then she carried the satchel into the kitchen and put it on the table. Mrs. Kreig was at the far end with her sewing and the lamp pulled over. "Your supper's in the warmer," she said. "It's been in the warmer since six." "In a minute." Lettie opened the satchel and set out the primer with the two slates she had brought back. The char across the base of her fingers came off onto the pale wood of the table. She turned the hand over, looked at it, left it alone. A pencil came up out of the satchel pocket, and a sheet of paper came out under it. At the head of the sheet she wrote Sam Jessup. Under that, Ada. She sat a moment with the pencil held above the paper, thinking of a room with a stove in it and a door that locked from the inside, then began the list of what could be done for those children by Monday, and when she had four things down she started on the fifth.


----- DRAFT 2 -----

"The children want you to see the foal," Cora Jessup said. "They have talked about nothing else since Tuesday." Lettie had six graded slates against her hip. She set them on the hall table, squared the stack with two fingers, and pulled the door wide. Cora stood on the step with her hands at her sides. The patch at her left elbow was gray thread on brown cloth, the stitches small and even, the work of somebody sewing at a low lamp. "It's a long ride out for a horse that can't do a thing yet," Cora said. "I told Burr you'd have better use for a Saturday." "I don't." The answer went out of Lettie before she had looked at it. A year of turning every sentence over first, and that one walked past her while she was holding the door. "He's a bay. Ruby's named him Admiral. Tom says that isn't a name for a horse." "It's a name for a horse with a good opinion of itself," Lettie said. Something crossed Cora's mouth and left again. "Come at noon, then. We haven't much to put in front of you, so don't arrive hungry." "I'll bring the primer. Tom can read to the foal." Behind her at the sideboard, Mrs. Kreig poured coffee and offered nothing at all on the subject. The wagon came up the road before ten with hay roped in the bed, four bales high and the rope run twice over the top of them. Nels pulled the team up short of the gate and got down to the knot. He worked it loose and took it up again a turn tighter while he talked. "That load's Burr Jessup's," he said. "I've owed it to him since April. His place sits a half mile off my road." "Then Miss Pardee can ride out with it," Cora said from the step. Nels put his shoulder against the wagon side and leaned his weight into it. The bales moved a hand's width and held. "It's rough past the ford. I'd have to go slow with somebody up." Being driven out of town by a man, in daylight, in front of a witness, broke two of the rules Lettie had written for Nell Pardee in her first week at the boarding house. She counted them and took the ride anyway. "I'd be obliged," she said. "Give me ten minutes." Mrs. Kreig came out with the pot still in her hand, set it on the porch rail, and went back inside. Lettie climbed up with her satchel and took hold of the seat rail. Nels kicked the brake lever off and gathered the lines, and the team came around out of the yard with the wagon creaking up under them. Dust rolled off the wheels and stood in the road behind. Roan Creek went past: the mercantile with its shutters still hooked back, Herrold's dog asleep on the boardwalk in the first good sun of the week, the church with a ladder against it and nobody on the ladder. Past the last fence Lettie let go of the rail and put her hands in her lap. The road climbed out of the bottomland and ran southwest into the foothills. Lupin stood thick in the meadow grass on both sides, blue running back from the ruts until the color broke up in the sage. Snow held on the high lines of the Bighorns. Lettie set her satchel down between her boots and turned on the seat to look at the country instead of the road. "That blue is lupin," Nels said. "Three weeks and it's gone. The grass goes brown behind it." "You sound sorry about it." "I am. It's the best three weeks in the year and there's nobody out here for them." He shifted the lines to one hand and pointed with the other, off at a cut in the hills where the green ran darker. "Water comes down there. You could put forty head in that draw and never haul a bucket." "You talk about it like it belongs to you." "It doesn't. That's most of why I like it." The cold of the night was coming up out of the ground as the sun took it, and the sage smelled of it, sharp, wet at the root. Nels held the lines across the seat. "Take them. They'll walk this stretch without help." Lettie took them. The leather was warm and stiffer than she expected, and it sat across palms the whole town had been remarking on since October. Mrs. Kreig had said it twice at supper, in the same words both times: schoolteacher's hands, no wear in them. "Higher," Nels said. "Let them feel it. You're not carrying them." "They're not carrying me either. Nobody is doing anything." "That's what driving is, most of the day." She held them out level and the near horse threw his head once and gave it up. "How far to the ford?" "Hour. Less if you keep them honest." She kept the lines and let the wagon go on under her. A meadowlark went up out of the lupin ahead of the team and came down again forty feet off, and the near horse took no interest in it at all. "Tom Jessup read to me on Thursday. Eleven pages of Cooper and never stopped for a word." "In February he wouldn't take the book off the table." "He wouldn't. So I quit asking him to read out loud in front of the others. He reads to me at the stove now while the rest of them do sums." "That's a trick." "It's a bribe. He gets the stove." Nels laughed once, low, and put his boot up on the footboard. "And the Herrold girl? Her father tells me you've ruined her." "Ada has eaten her lunch with the Jessups and the Voight girl every day for a month. Her father sent me a note about it." "Saying what?" "Asking whether the noon hour could be arranged differently." "What did you write back?" "That the noon hour is arranged by the children." He looked over at her for a second longer than the road allowed, then back at the team. Lettie kept the lines. The wagon dropped through a dry wash and came up the far side. Ahead of them the meadow ran out blue, then gray-green, then blue again where the ground dipped. "How long have you," she said, and stopped, and started again. "Is the ford bad this late?" "Down some. It'll wet the hubs." They ran another mile on the grass and the water and the price of lumber in Sheridan. She had said three or four things without weighing any of them first, and the count was what told her she had stopped weighing. Nels reached over and took the lines back without looking at her, still talking about the ford. The near wheel dropped into a washed-out rut below the crossing and the wagon went over hard. Lettie came off the board seat an inch and caught his forearm with both hands. Muscle under the sleeve. Heat through the cloth. The jolt was still coming up through the seat when she let go. "They fill that in every spring," Nels said. "It comes back every spring." "Mm." The team took the pull up out of the bottom, and the harness leather creaked and settled. Lettie put her hand in her lap. It stayed half closed, and she left it there. They came over the last rise and the Jessup place opened below them: the house, the paddock rails, the well with its bucket hung on the post. Where the barn had stood, four posts came up black out of the grass. The rest lay in the yard in the rows it had fallen in, timbers burned through in the middle and thick at the ends, the ground under them bare and not yet green. A lean-to of new lumber stood against the corral fence, raw and pale, the sawn ends still yellow. Nels swung the team wide and backed the wagon to the lean-to. Lettie climbed down and crossed the yard, and at the nearest standing post she stopped and set her hand flat against it. Char came away on her palm, soft, and under it the wood was still solid. Six weeks on, the smell was in everything, in the grass, in the rails, in the air over the whole yard. The children came out around the house at a run and then slowed, all three of them, before they reached her. "Miss Pardee, he's Admiral," Ruby said. She had the lead rope in both fists. The foal came along behind it on legs he was still deciding about, and the halter hung loose enough to turn on his head. "He isn't," Tom said. "Ma hasn't said." "He's got a white foot." "One white foot," Lettie said. "Admiral will do until he earns something better." Ruby thought about that with her chin down. Behind her, the smallest one, Ned, stood with his hand on the paddock rail and looked at the wagon rather than the foal. Ruby put the lead in Ned's hand and took it back before he had it. Tom stood off from all of it with his boots in the burned ground, on the black, where the grass had not come up, and he stayed there until Lettie said his name. "The stove on Monday," she said. "If the book comes with you." Ruby got the foal turned and the three of them went off toward the paddock with Nels behind them, and Lettie was left with the char on her hand. She closed it and looked at the house. Cora had the door open. "Come in out of it." The kitchen was swept and dim and smelled of bread. Cora took a loaf out of the oven, turned it out of the pan onto a cloth, and set the pan back on the stove before she said anything else. Her hands stayed steady through every part of it. "It was the second week of April," she said. "They came about one in the morning. Four of them, on horses, and I couldn't tell you a face. "He broke this ground with a hand plow and a borrowed mule and he will be in it before he signs it over. I've stopped asking. I ask and it takes something out of him I can't put back." It went through under her thumb, and then the wagon came down off a rut and knocked it open again. "That road wants a grader," Nels said. "It wants a county." They went down the long grade above the ford. Lettie braced her boot against the footboard and felt Nels put his hand to the brake lever at the same time, the two of them leaning back off the same slope. "Cold tonight," she said. "Sage will be up in the morning." He let the brake off at the bottom and the team took the wagon up onto the flat, and neither of them started anything after that. With both arms over the satchel, Lettie watched the road come at them in the last of the light. He set her down at the boarding house gate. The windows were yellow and Mrs. Kreig's shape went across one of them. "Monday, then," Nels said. "Monday." He backed the team a length to clear the gatepost, turned in the road, and went. Lettie stood at the gate with her satchel against her leg until the wagon noise was down past the church. The hall table had a fresh stack of slates on it. Lettie looked once up the stairs, at the door of the room where the trunk sat locked under the window, then carried her satchel through to the kitchen and put it on the table. "There's supper back for you," Mrs. Kreig said from the stove. A plate went onto the warming shelf with a saucer over it. "You'll have it while it's hot or you'll have it cold, and I know which." Lettie opened the satchel. She set out the primer and four slates in a row, took up a pencil, and turned the char mark on her palm over once under the lamp. Then she wrote Tom Jessup at the head of the first slate, and Ruby under that, and Ned under that, and started on the list of what she could put in their hands by Monday morning.


----- DRAFT 3 -----

"The children want you to see the foal." Cora Jessup stood on the boarding house porch in the Saturday light with her hands loose at her sides. Lettie had been grading slates at the hall table. She carried the stack with her, set it on the sill, and opened the door wider. "When?" "Today, if you can spare it. She came Tuesday. They've talked of nothing since and I'm past listening." "I can spare today." "It's four miles out. Burr has the wagon down at the field till noon." The patch at Cora's elbow was stitched small and even, dark thread on faded blue, the cloth beneath it worn to a shine. "I've walked four miles." "You'll not walk it in that skirt." Ottilie Kreig came along the passage with the coffee pot, filled a cup at the sideboard, and set the pot down. She went back toward the kitchen without a word. The wagon came up the street while they stood there. Nels Carrick had three bales roped into the bed and the near rope had gone slack over the top one. He pulled up at the gate, climbed down, put a boot to the hub, and hauled the knot tight against the load. "Mrs. Jessup." He touched his hat brim. "Mr. Carrick." "I'm carrying your hay out this morning. Your husband said Saturday." "Saturday," Cora said. He came around the horses and looked up at the porch. "Miss Pardee. If you're going out to the Jessup place, there's a seat and it's on my road." Lettie glanced up the street, at the store front, at the window of the front bedroom where a curtain hung still. She had a rule about being seen anywhere with anyone, and she heard herself set it down. "Then I'd be glad of the seat." She came out to the wagon with her satchel over her arm. Nels put his weight against the wheel while she climbed, and when she was up he went back to the near side, released the brake, and spoke to the team. The store fronts slid past. Dust came up yellow off the wheels and hung in the road behind them. Somewhere past the church she let go of the seat rail. The road left the last fence and climbed, and the meadow came up on both sides of it, blue with lupin. "That shouldn't be out yet," Nels said. "Cold as it's been." "It doesn't seem to know." "No. It doesn't." He shifted the lines into one hand and pointed north, where the Bighorns stood with snow still lying in the high draws. "You can read the lines from here. That one under the shoulder is where the creek comes from in July. When it's gone off that rock, the Jessups' well drops eight inches and Burr starts watching the sky." "You know a good deal about their well." "I know a good deal about every well between here and the pass. It's what there is to talk about at the store, along with the price of beef and who has died." He let the team walk. "A man came through in the fall wanting to sell me a wind pump out of a catalogue. Forty dollars, freight extra, and it would not have lasted one winter off that ridge." "Did you tell him so?" "I bought a bucket instead and he took it hard." The grade steepened and the wagon creaked under them and the smell came up off the ground, sage after a cold night, warm dust, harness leather. "I came out here for the graze," he said. "I stayed for the light. It does something at this elevation in the last hour that it doesn't do anywhere I've been." "Is that a reason a man gives at the land office?" "It is not." He laughed, one short sound. "Hold these a minute." He put the lines into her hands. She took them and he showed her where to keep them, off the near horse's neck, and then he swung back over the seat and worked the rope on the hay, drew the slack through, and set the knot with his heel braced on the sideboard. The horses walked on. Lettie held the leather across her palms, watched their ears, and kept the lines clear of the near horse's neck. Her hands were still the wrong hands for this country, soft across the base of the fingers, and the town had noticed them in April and had gone on noticing. "You've had them straighter than I do," he said, coming back over. "They're doing it themselves." "That's the whole trade." He took the lines back and settled them across his knuckles. "Twelve in the school now?" "Twelve when the weather holds. Nine when it doesn't, and six in October when there's beets to lift." "You'll not get the six." "I'll get them in the evening if I have to go and stand in the field and wait for them." She put her chin toward the ridge. "Tell me what that is." "Elk Tooth. There's a spring under the north side of it that runs all summer, and the whole basin would have been settled ten years sooner if anybody had gone up there and found it." He looked at it while the horses walked. "Mrs. Kreig says you've got Burr's boy reading." "He read a page Thursday." "He hadn't been." "He'd sat eleven days with a slate and never marked it once. I put Cooper in front of him, the Mohicans, and let him alone. He got to the bottom of the page before he knew he'd done it. Then he looked up to see whether I'd noticed." "Had you?" "I gave him the next page." Nels made a sound low in his chest that was mostly agreement. The road bent and the meadow ran out ahead of them, unfenced, blue and green to the base of the foothills. "The Scutt girl carried two apples out at noon on Wednesday," Lettie said. "For a month she made the homesteaders' children sit at the back wall and eat where she couldn't see them. Now she carries two apples and eats one." "What changed her?" "I moved her desk." "That's all of it?" "I moved it three times." The buckle of her satchel had come open under her thumb. She fastened it, and unfastened it, and somewhere in there she understood that she had stopped composing her sentences before she let them out, and that she had been saying them for two or three miles. She fastened the buckle again and left it. "In Missouri," he said, "did you have a school with a floor in it, or ..." He let it go by himself. Lettie watched the near horse's ears. "The roof at the school still leaks," she said. "Over the stove, which is the one place a roof shouldn't." "I'll bring shingles Tuesday." The near wheel dropped into a rut at the bottom of the grade and the wagon went over hard to the left. Lettie caught his arm above the elbow. The muscle came up tight under the sleeve and the heat of him went through the cloth into her hand. She let go and put the hand back in her lap. "Road washes there," Nels said. "Every spring, and every spring I mean to bring a shovel." "Yes." She kept the hand where it was for the next mile, fingers closed a little, holding the shape of his forearm. They came in past the corral, and Lettie saw the barn. Six posts stood up out of the black ground with nothing between them. The timbers had burned down to a coal skin that had split and curled in the weather, and the ground around them was still bare where the heat had gone into it. A length of chain lay half in the ash with its links welded together. Somebody had raked the yard side of the burn into a low ridge and left the rake standing in it. Off past that stood a lean-to of raw new boards, eight feet deep, roofed with tin, and empty. Lettie climbed down and set her hand flat against one of the standing posts. It gave under her palm, and when she took the hand away the char had come off on it in a black print across the base of her fingers. Nels backed the wagon to the lean-to and had the first bale off before the children reached the yard. There were three of them. They came at a run, and the smallest arrived first because the other two stopped to argue. "She's called Blossom," the girl said. "She is not," said the middle one. "Blossom is a cow's name. She's General." "Miss Pardee, tell her that's a horse for a general." "She's a filly," Lettie said. "You've a while yet to settle it." They took her out to the paddock and put the lead in her hand and then took it back, and the filly stood with a halter on that was three sizes past her, the cheek straps hanging in loops beside her jaw. The mare put herself between the filly and the fence and stayed there. "That's Burr's father's halter," the girl said. "There isn't a small one." "Then she'll grow into it." "That's what he said." The Jessup boy came up last from the field with his hat in his hand and stood at the rail without saying anything, and when Nels asked him to hold the mare's head he went in and held it. Cora was at the kitchen door with a cloth over her shoulder. "Come in out of that sun a minute." Inside, she turned a loaf out of its crock onto the board and knocked the crock clean and set it upside down. She poured two cups and put one at Lettie's hand. "You've seen the barn." "Yes." "Then I'll tell it and be done with it, because Burr won't, and the children have it in pieces." Cora took the cloth off her shoulder and folded it. "They came the twelfth of the month. After midnight. Four horses. I heard them on the road before I heard them in the yard, and I knew what it was before I was out of the bed." "How many men?" "I saw three at the fence. There was another one holding at the road, and he was the one giving it." She turned the loaf over and set the crock down beside it. "They never said a word to us. Not one. They fired the hay and stood off and watched it take, and when Burr came out with the shotgun they let him come. That's what I keep. They let him come out. They weren't afraid of him at all." "Did he fire?" "He had no target that would hold still and he knew it. He stood in his union suit in the middle of his own yard and he watched two hundred dollars of winter feed go up, and one of them said the only thing that was said all night." Cora's hands were steady on the loaf, turning it, setting it square on the board. "He said there was paper coming and it would be easier if we were gone before it got here. Then they rode off south. Not toward town." "And the sheriff?" "Rode out Thursday. Walked the burn. Wrote it in a book." Cora set the cups straight on the table. "He said there'd been fires up in the Powder country as well, and he asked me twice had we quarreled with anybody. I told him we haven't quarreled with anybody in nine years. He wrote that down too." "Did he ask about the paper?" "I didn't tell him about the paper." She looked at the window. "Burr said not to. He said a thing like that goes into a book and then it goes around a table at the hotel, and by Sunday it would be Burr Jessup saying somebody means to run him off, and by the Sunday after that it would be Burr Jessup drinking." "Will Burr sell?" "Burr won't leave." She said it flat. "He was born in Ohio in a house his father lost. He's put nine years in this ground and he will die on it before he signs, and I have known that about him since I married him, and I married him for it." She set the cloth down. "I'm afraid, Miss Pardee. I'm afraid at four in the afternoon standing in my own kitchen, and I don't know how much longer afraid is a thing I can manage." Lettie sat with her hands around the cup. "Do you sleep?" "I sleep from two till about four. Then the house makes a noise." "Which of the children knows?" "The boy knows all of it. He was at the window. The girls know there was a fire." Cora pushed the loaf a half inch to square it with the edge of the board. "That's why I wanted you out here. They've had a fire and a hard spring and a mother who jumps at a door. I wanted them to have a Saturday with a foal in it and their teacher standing in the yard." Lettie put her hand across the table and took Cora's, and Cora let her. The two of them sat there with the loaf steaming between them and the sound of the children out past the wall, and Lettie held on. The steam went off the loaf. She was holding on still when the boy shouted something out past the wall. Nels had the lean-to full by three. Burr came up from the field while the last bale was going in and stood at the tailgate with his thumbs in his belt. "I'll settle for it in the fall," he said. "Settle for it whenever." "I'll settle in the fall, Carrick." "That'll do." Burr put his hand on the top bale and pressed his fingers into it, testing the tie, and stood there a moment with his weight on the wagon. "You'd have got a better price for it in town," he said. "I'd have got a price. I wouldn't have got it hauled." Burr took his hand off the bale and went into the house. Lettie said goodbye to the children at the paddock rail. The middle one had the lead again and was walking the filly in a circle nobody had asked for. "Monday," Lettie said. "And I'll want the second page from you," she said to the boy, "not the first one over again." "Yes, ma'am." The wagon was loud on the way back with nothing in the bed. The sun had come around and the Bighorns stood dark on the right of them, and the light was doing what he had said it did. "Rope's staying," Nels said. "You tied it twice." "I tied it three times. Once for the hill." He coiled the loose end against his knee as they went and dropped it behind the seat. "There's a better road along the creek, only it's under water till June." "Then this is the road." "This is the road." They went a mile without saying anything more. Lettie worked the buckle of her satchel open and closed on her knees, and on one of the downgrades she braced her boot against the footboard and held it there through the pitch, and she understood, with her foot pushed against the board and her shoulder an inch from his, that he had come out of that yard carrying the same thing she was carrying. He set her down at the gate at dusk. "Tuesday," he said. "Shingles." "There's a hole the size of a hat over the stove." "Then I'll bring two bundles." He turned the team and went up the street, and Lettie stood a moment at the gate with her satchel under her arm. The char was still across the base of her fingers. She looked at it, and went in. Ottilie had the lamp lit in the kitchen and a plate of supper set back on the warming shelf. "You'll eat that before it turns to leather." "In a minute." Ottilie looked at the black print across Lettie's fingers and got the soap dish down off the shelf and set it by the basin, and said nothing at all about the wagon or the hour or the four miles. Lettie glanced at the stairs, at the dark of the landing above them, and then she put her satchel on the kitchen table and opened it. The primer came out first and went aside. She took out a clean sheet and the pencil. Under the lamp, with the supper going cold at her elbow, she wrote the Jessup boy's name at the top of the sheet, and the girls' names under it. Then she began the list of what she would need by Monday, and beside each item she wrote down who in Roan Creek she would have to ask for it.
