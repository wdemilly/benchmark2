STEP 2A — ASSESS THREE DRAFTS AND SELECT ONE
(v1 — 2026-07-18. New step. Purpose: the same chapter is drafted three times by Step 2; this step reads all three as literature and selects the one to keep. This is a craft judgment, not a measurement. Derived from a close comparison of three Keystone chapter 2 drafts whose quality differences were large and identifiable; the criteria below are the differences that mattered there. To be run in a fresh incognito window with no other context. Amended 21 July 2026 on Walter's instruction: criteria 3 and 4 are swapped, so dialogue under load is now judged third and information strategy fourth. Reason: on Keystone chapter 10b the old order selected the draft that scored 84 over the draft that scored 91, and the assessment itself named the cause — it ranked the winning draft best on dialogue and then said the criterion order broke the tie against it. Under the new order it selects the 91.)

You are a fiction editor reading three drafts of the same chapter. All three were written from the same outline. Your job is to judge them as prose fiction — as an acquiring editor would — and select the strongest. You are not checking them against the outline for coverage; assume all three cover it.

Read all three drafts completely before writing anything.

Then assess each draft against these criteria, in this order. For every judgment you make, quote the exact passage it rests on. A judgment without a quotation does not count.

1. INTERIORITY. When the chapter goes inside the point-of-view character, is the interior material pinned to specific remembered places, objects, and people, or does it run to summary and abstraction? A memory with a name and a physical detail outranks a reflection that tells the reader what to conclude. Quote the best-pinned interior moment and the most abstract one in each draft.

2. THEME HANDLING. Does any character state the chapter's theme out loud? A speech that announces the meaning of the story is a defect, however well written. Behavior that carries the meaning without naming it is the standard. Quote the most thesis-like line in each draft and the best moment where meaning is carried by action alone.

3. DIALOGUE UNDER LOAD. Find the exchange that carries the most story weight in each draft. Does the dialogue do more than one job at a time — advance the plot, reveal the speaker, and obstruct the other character in the same lines? Quote the single best line of dialogue in each draft and say what jobs it is doing.

4. INFORMATION STRATEGY. What does each draft withhold, and how? The strongest choice lets the reader catch something a character misses, staged in plain sight without narration pointing at it. The weakest explains its own secrets. Identify each draft's central withheld fact and quote how it is planted.

5. MOTIF DISCIPLINE. Does a physical gesture or object recur with accumulated meaning — the same action returning later as metaphor, earned rather than installed? Quote the recurrence if it exists.

6. PRECISION OF THE PHYSICAL WORLD. Are measurements, procedures, and objects exact, consistent, and professionally credible throughout? Note any place where the physical facts blur, contradict, or go generic.

7. LINE FAULTS. Note clumsy constructions, repeated sentence shapes, tense wobbles, and dead phrases, with quotations. Weigh these last; a draft with the best architecture and a few rough lines beats a clean draft with weak architecture.

Then write the verdict:

- Rank the three drafts, first to third.
- For the winner, state in one paragraph why it wins, resting the paragraph on the quoted evidence above.
- For each loser, state in one or two sentences the decisive weakness.
- If two drafts are close, say so and say what would break the tie.
- Name the winner on its own final line, exactly: SELECTED: DRAFT [number].

Do not summarize the plot. Do not suggest revisions. Do not comment on the outline. Judge what is on the page.

----- DRAFT 1 -----

The eighth block on the skid answered wrong under the mallet. Verena struck it again, lower on the face, and the sound came back short and dead. Behind her the hoist creaked on the abutment platform and two men argued over a sling. She put her shoulder into the block until it rolled a quarter turn on the timbers. She took the dipper off the water butt, threw half of it across the stone, and crouched while the wet was still bright to read the bed. Sap streaks ran through the line of it, wide and pale. Her thumbnail went down one and came away with a smear of paste on it. Ring first, water second, nail last. Elias had tested in that order for thirty years, and she was three steps into it before she chose any of them. She wiped her thumb on her apron and stood, the chalk keel knocking against her hip as she came up. "Emory." She pitched it over the hoist. "Come and hit this." Emory Sault came off the north stack at a walk, gloves under one arm, the only man in Cordell willing to work this bridge and in no hurry about it. "I've hauled that one twice this morning," he said. "It doesn't care for me either." She put the mallet in his hand. "Strike the face. Then strike the one behind it." He struck it square. The block gave back the same flat nothing it had given her. He moved along the skid without being told and hit the next one, and that one rang clean and went on ringing. "Well," Emory said. "Yes." "Cracked?" "Green. Cut this spring, and dressed by somebody in a hurry." She took the mallet back off him. "Frost would have it out of a wall in one winter. A hammer would have it sooner." "How much of the load is that way?" "That is what we are doing next." She got the order book out of her apron and walked the delivery from the head of the skids, the book braced open against her thigh, the pencil stub between two fingers. Sound stone took an S at the corner, backing-grade a B on the same face. The rest took an X, drawn heavy enough to see from across the yard, and by the fourth wagon-load she was drawing more X than anything else. "Kittrell's outfit brought it," Emory said. He came along behind her with his thumbs in his belt. "Four wagons. Off the tailboards before six." "I ordered from Dunmore." She marked a stone at the knee and moved on. "Ticket says Kittrell hauling for Dunmore." "Who signed it?" "I did. You were down at the tent with the timber man." He waited while she wrote. "Nobody else was up yet." "Did you walk the load before you signed?" "It was dark and it was stone." He rubbed the back of his neck. "At that hour it is all stone." She crossed out a block dressed with a claw where a boaster should have gone, and stepped back to see the whole run of them at once. Sixty ordered against the schedule, and nineteen she would put in a bearing course, four of those nineteen worth arguing over with a man who knew the trade. "Who was driving?" "Kittrell hires by the day. Could have been anybody with a hand free and a hat." The tally at the foot of the column came out wrong twice. She struck the figure through, wrote it lower down, underlined it, and stood with the pencil stopped on the paper. Every block she had crossed out was half a day off the completion clause, and the clause carried a date that would not move for weather or for green stone. The tarped chest sat at the yard's edge in the lee of the rubble stack. She wanted a finer edge for the arris work, so she threw the tarp off and lifted the lid, and the tools lay bedded in Elias's order, largest to finest, edges to the left, as she had found them in the trunk and never once rearranged them. The inch-and-a-quarter came up with the handle sprung at the ferrule. She fitted it, tapped it on the block face, and felt the head rock a sixteenth under the blow. She held the ferrule level with her eye and turned it to the light. The split ran two inches down the ash from the collar, and the wood inside was pale and dry, no oil worked into it, no dust, no black where a season's grit would sit. Heat alone could do it. A handle shrinks in a dry July and the collar goes slack and the head starts to walk. She ran her thumb around the ferrule below the split, and the collar was tight there and tight all round, as tight as it had sat when she laid the chisel in the chest at the end of yesterday's shift, after cutting the springer bed with it all afternoon. "Do you want the sound ones stacked separate?" Emory called from the skids. "Separate, and under tarp." She kept her eyes on the ferrule. "Go on with it now." He went off up the skids at his own pace. She took the chest apart and laid the pieces along the plank: the two-pound, the pitching tool, the point, the plumb bob in its rag, the boaster, the templet. The plumb bob's cord was wound as she had wound it and the templet sat true against the plank. Everything lay in the order Elias had left it in, every handle worn to a grip wider than her own. She put the sprung chisel back on the felt and dropped the lid. The tarp came over and a waste block went on the corner to hold it. Her own chisel went through her belt, and she walked up the yard with the order book open in her hand and gave the crew the day's work in the voice she had used since May. "Anything marked S goes up to the platform. Backing stone to the north stack, and stack it low. Crossed stone stays where it lies until I say." Farrow asked about the second course and got his answer before he had finished asking it. "Nothing goes near the springer that I have not marked myself." From the rim road, where a man could sit a horse and look down for as long as he liked, it would show as a yard working a delivery on a hot morning. She had got very good at that. Merritt Ballard came up from the falsework at two, and he was fifty feet off when he changed his line and went to the crossed stone instead of to her. He crouched to the skid, weight off the bad leg, and got his good hand under a marked block and stood it on edge. He held the bed up to the light and read it. He set the block down again without hurry and looked over at her, and what he asked for was the paperwork. "Who signed the ticket?" "Emory. Before six, in the dark." "You ordered Dunmore." "The paper says Dunmore. The wagons say Kittrell, hauling for him." She came down the skid to where he was. "Kittrell hires his drivers by the day." "How many sound?" "Nineteen of sixty. Four of those I would fight you over." Merritt looked up the yard at the abutment, at the springer bed cut and waiting with nothing to set on it. "That is your week." "That is my week and two days of the next. Dunmore cannot cut and dress a fresh lot inside of nine days, and I have five." "Ask him anyway." "I sent Farrow down at noon." She took the chisel out of her apron and held it out to him handle first. He turned it over with his good hand, the crushed one resting on his knee, and he brought the ferrule up to the light without being told what to look at. "Yesterday?" "All yesterday afternoon. Sound in my hand at six o'clock." "Wood is white in there." He turned it again. "There is no dirt in that split. It has not stood open a day." "Nobody comes up here but us." "Then it is somebody who knows which chest is yours and where the tarp is weighted." He handed it back. "Say nothing to the crew." "I was not going to." "No." He got his hand on the skid and came up off the leg. "You would not." They made the afternoon out of what was sound. Merritt worked the stacks from the north end and she worked from the south, and they chalked as they went, and the chalk decided the week between them. "Nineteen holds the voussoirs," she said. "Nineteen holds the voussoirs and nothing else. Bearing course out of the eleven backing-grade?" "Not on the bearing course. Backing and fill, where nothing thrusts through it." She squatted at the springer with the templet against the cut. "Pascoe's rule was never to spend good stone where bad will do the same work." "Pascoe's rule." Merritt said it flat, and he was looking at the templet and not at her. "It leaves you a thin week." "It leaves me a week." Emory came past with a backing block on the barrow and the sweat running out of his hat. "If a man wanted us stopped he could have sent nothing at all," he said. "Cheaper, and we would be no worse off." "Take that one to the north stack." "He would have had my signature for nothing, though," Emory said, and went on with the barrow. They re-sorted the stacks first, backing-grade to the north where the barrow run was short, the nineteen laid up on skids under their own tarp with a man's coat over the corner to hold it. Merritt chalked V on the voussoir stone, one to eleven, and she went along behind him and checked the numbers against the templet before the chalk dried. "Eight is a hair shy on the bed," she said. "Then eight goes in the fill and we are down to ten." "Ten and the springer. It carries." They set a short course before the light went, to prove the plan on the wall and not on paper. Verena spread the bed while Merritt dropped the plumb-line and held it steady off the springer. She brought the first block down onto the mortar and tapped it home along the line with the handle of the mallet. It came to the line and stayed. The second sat proud a quarter inch and she tapped it down and it stayed too. "Line," Merritt said. "Hold it there." Four blocks, and the course ran true, and the deadline lived through another afternoon by a margin thinner than the one she had woken up with. At five a rider came along the rim road above the works, walking a bay horse with the dust going up slow behind him. Merritt was crouched at the barrow with a chalked block in his hand. He straightened, took two steps that put nothing between him and the road but his own back, and settled his weight there. Verena went on cutting. The chisel took the arris off in a line and the dust came away fine and white and went down her forearm. "Hand me that springer block when you have finished it," Merritt said. "It wants another minute." "I will wait on it." The rider went along the rim and out of sight past the shoulder of the gorge. Merritt stayed where he was until she had finished the arris and handed the block up to him, and then he took his time setting it down. The crew knocked off at seven. Emory went by with his gloves in his fist and his shirt over his shoulder. "Do not sleep up here," he said. "Goodnight, Emory." She dragged the tarp over the fresh course and weighted the four corners with waste, tipped the bucket out into the rubble, and hung it on the butt. The long light had gone up onto the far rim and left the yard in shadow with the heat still coming off the stone. She walked the stacks a last time with her hand out, counting the sound blocks by touch as she passed them. Nineteen of them, and no more coming before Thursday. She got to the end of the row and the yard was quiet and there was nobody left in it to hold anything for. She went to the chest and threw the tarp back and took out her father's chisel. The plank across the trestle was still wet from the bucket. She wiped it down with her sleeve, laid the chisel across it, and took up the pincers. The ferrule came off the ash a sixteenth at a time, worked round and round, and it came away clean and unsprung, which meant the collar had never been the trouble. She stood it on the plank on end. The wedge in the head took three taps of the small hammer before it stood up out of the slot, and she set the hammer down beside it and looked at the two of them lying there, both of them sound, both of them out of a chisel she could not put in a block. The split ran with the grain, and the ash in it was good ash. A wedge driven crooked would open a handle like that, and a pry bar worked under the collar would open it, and nothing else on this rim would. She turned the handle over in her fingers. The wood was worn hollow under the collar where a wider thumb than hers had ridden it for thirty years, and her own thumb sat down into the hollow and found it wrong, wrong every day since May, and she had gone on working through it every one of those days. The wedge went out of the slot and onto the plank. She stopped with the head half loose in her hand. She sat down on a backing block at the end of the plank and put the chisel in her lap and left it there. Below the yard the gorge had gone blue to the water and the swallows were out over it. Her shoulders came down off the working set she had carried since six that morning. Her hands stopped where they were, one round the head, one round the split handle, and she let them stop. "Elias," she said. The name went out across the yard and along the stacks, and there was nobody left in the works to hear her say it. She sat there a while longer with the light going off the rim. Then she took the oiled rag out of the chest, wound it round the chisel head and the split handle both, and laid the tool back in its place in his order, edge to the left. The wedge and the ferrule went into the felt beside it where she would find them in the morning. The lid came down on all of it. She drew the tarp over the chest, squared it at the corners, and set the waste block back to hold it, and she had her own chisel out of her belt before she was clear of the rubble stack. Tomorrow's springer was standing at the head of the skids with the last of the light still on its face. She laid the templet across it and drew the cut line with the keel, and squared the line a second time, harder, since that was the one she would work to at six in the morning. The chalk stood up white on the grey and would still be there at first light for anyone who cared to look at what the yard meant to do next. "You will do," she told the block, and moved the templet down to the second one. She would say nothing about any of it. Not to Emory, not to Farrow, not to Sabra when she came up on Sunday with the mail and her questions. The loss would go into the schedule and be buried there. The yard would run an ordinary week, and the man who had cut the falsework a year ago, and who had come in here yesterday with a pry bar for her father's chest, would see nothing in it to worry him. She put the keel back in her apron and started down toward the wagon road, working out as she went where a person could stand at six in the morning to see every face on every wagon, and who signed, and who stood by to watch it done.


----- DRAFT 2 -----

The load had been set down on the skids before six, and Verena Pascoe went along it with a mallet before any of it got near the abutment. She struck the first block on the shoulder and got back the hard flat note she wanted, a sound with an edge that ran out to the arris and stopped clean. The second gave her the same. The third answered wrong. She hit it again in the same place. The note came up short and soft and died somewhere in the middle of the block. She laid her palm flat against the face and struck a third time and felt the deadness come back through her wrist. Emory Sault was up on the stack sorting backing. Verena took the water bucket off the plank, tipped a cupful across the face of the third block, and watched the wet go. It ran quick in bands where the grain lay open and stood in beads where it did not. She set her thumbnail to the bed line and dragged it the length of the block. Fine crumb came away under the nail, damp the whole way through. Green stone. Out of the ground weeks ago, not months, and dressed by somebody working fast. "Emory. Come down here." He came off the stack with a hand on the skid. She put the mallet in his palm and pointed with two fingers at the second block, then the third. "Hit that one. Then hit that one." He hit the second and looked at her. He hit the third, and his shoulders came up off it. "That's gone flat." "It has." "Is it cracked through?" "No. Wet." She took the mallet back and hung it through her belt. "It came out of the quarry too soon and it is still carrying quarry water. Wet stone dresses easy. Cuts like cheese, and a man in a hurry loves it." Emory put his boot against the block and rocked it on the timber. "So what does it do?" "Dries in a wall. Shrinks. The face spalls off in the second frost and somebody rebuilds my course three winters from now." Verena went up the yard for the order book and came back down the line with it braced open against her thigh. The keel was in her apron pocket where it lived, a stub of red chalk worn off to a wedge on one side. She put a single stroke on the first block. A single stroke on the second. A cross on the third, hard enough to leave grit under her thumb. "Who brought it in?" "Teamster out of Ridgely." Emory took the far side of the skids and came along with her. "Not Hallett. Come up about four with two wagons and a boy driving the second." "Did you sign?" "You were down at the pier. I signed the tally. I never looked at the stone." "What did the tally say?" "Forty-two ashlar, twelve backing. That's what came off the wagons." She marked and moved and marked, and the crosses came faster than the strokes did. By the eleventh block she had stopped needing the mallet, because the bad ones took the water differently and she could read them standing four feet off. At the end of the line she straightened her back and let it out. "Forty-two came off. Nineteen of them are sound." Emory stopped where he was. "Nineteen." She braced the book against her thigh and set the pencil stub to the tally column. Forty-two struck out, nineteen written under it. The backing was worse, seven of twelve, and she struck that figure out and wrote the new one beside it. Thirty-one sound ashlar was what the week's course wanted laid and dressed. She underlined the nineteen twice and left the pencil sitting in the fold of the book. Every block on that skid with a cross on it came off the far end of the schedule, off the date Ambrose Corliss had written into the completion clause in a hand as neat as a wedding invitation. Eleven weeks was what she had this morning. Standing here she had eleven weeks less four days, and nobody in the territory knew it but her. The finer work on the springer wanted a chisel she had not carried down. Verena crossed to the tool chest at the yard's edge, pulled the block off the corner of the tarp, threw the canvas back and lifted the lid. Her father's tools lay in his order. Points and boasters on the left, ranged by width, then the drafting chisels, then the pitching tool with the mushroomed head he had never let anyone grind. She took out the three-quarter and knew before it was clear of the tray. The handle moved on the steel. She sat back on her heel and fitted it properly, thumb and two fingers, and tapped the head twice against the block beside her. The wood rocked at the ferrule and rocked again. She turned the tool over into the long light coming across the rim. The handle was split along the grain from the ferrule down, an inch and a half of it, and the wood inside the split was pale and clean and dry. She had used this chisel yesterday afternoon on the springer draft. She had wiped it and laid it in the chest herself before she walked up to Sabra's. The ordinary answer was right there for the taking: heat, a dry week, a lid dropped hard on the handle by a man in a hurry. Verena put the ferrule back into the light. No bruise in the wood, no crush at the edges of the split, no dust worked down inside it. That pale interior had not stood open in this yard for an hour. She went through the chest item by item. Every handle, every ferrule, the mallet head, the templet, the two plumb bobs, the coiled line. Nothing else had been touched. The tools sat exactly as Elias Pascoe had left them, rubbed dark where his grip had been, every hollow in the wrong place for her hand. "Do you want the sound stone stacked separate?" Emory called from the skids. "Stack it separate. Under the tarp. Do it now." He went. Verena stayed down on her heel a moment longer with the chisel across her knee. Then she laid it in the chest, dropped the lid, and set the block back on the corner of the tarp. The crew got the day from her in plain working order. All twelve green blocks to the fill behind the north wing wall, where nothing bore and nothing thrust. The nineteen sound ashlar carried up to the abutment face. Barrows first, then the mortar, then the line. She gave it to them one job at a time and explained none of it. Her own chisel came off the rack and went through her belt. Nobody asked about the chest. From the rim road, a hundred and forty feet up and half a mile off, the works would show a crew carrying stone to a face and a woman in a canvas apron marking blocks with chalk. That was what she wanted showing. She had been putting that face on since May. She was better at it now than she cared to be. Merritt Ballard came up from the toll road after two, with the heat standing on the rim and the crew gone quiet under it. He walked the skids without being asked and stopped at the crossed blocks. He got one up off the timber one-handed, knee under it, and set it down again and looked at the water mark still drying on the face. "Where did this come out of?" "Ridgely wagons. Half four yesterday." "You order it?" "I ordered forty-two ashlar out of Coyle's, cut and dressed to my templet." Verena set the order book down on the block. "That is not Coyle's stone and it is not dressed to anything." Merritt crouched by the skid with the weight off the bad leg and put his good hand flat on the block. "How much of it is rubbish?" "Twenty-three of the forty-two. Five of the twelve backing." "What does that cost you?" "Four days, if the rest of the summer behaves itself." She went to the chest, came back with her father's chisel, and put it in his hand. He turned it once. He got his thumb under the ferrule and rocked the handle, and his face did not shift at all. "When did you use it last?" "Yesterday, on the springer. It was sound and I put it away sound." He held the split up to the light and looked down into it. "Clean." "Yes." "Anybody in the yard overnight?" "The chest sits ten feet off the track up to your toll. Anybody at all." Merritt handed the tool back. "Bad stone is a mistake a supplier makes once and swears about. A handle is not." "No." "Then somebody stood in this yard last night working out how to cost you a week you could not explain." "He would have had more than that, if a green block had gone up in the bearing course and I had not caught it." Down at the wing wall a mallet started, and Emory said something to the Ridgely men that made one of them laugh into his sleeve. "Who have you told?" Merritt said. "You." They took the week apart on the plank table with the templet and the chalk. Merritt held the order book open under the heel of his bad hand and read the counts off while she wrote the new course out on the boards in keel. "Nineteen sound," she said. "The springers take four. Voussoirs want eleven when we get that far." "That leaves four for the face." "It leaves four, and the face wants nine this week." "Then the face waits and the backing goes up behind it." "The backing goes up." She dragged the chalk line down the plank and squared the end of it. "Green stone behind the wing, all of it, where it can shrink until Christmas and do nothing about anything. Sound stone comes off that stack for bearing work and for nothing else." "You will have a beautiful wall that nobody can see." "My father never spent a sound block where a bad one would hold. I had it off him about four hundred times." They re-sorted the stacks while the light held and set a short course on the ground to prove the plan would carry. Verena spread the mortar bed and struck it off level. Emory brought the block down on the barrow and Merritt took the far corner into his shoulder as it came off, and they walked it onto the bed together. She dropped the plumb-line off the springer and held it steady while Emory shifted the block a half inch. She tapped it down to the line with the wooden dresser, laid the templet across the bed joint, and tapped again at the tail. "That's level." "It will do." Emory stood back and wiped his face on his forearm. "Grand. Four blocks. At this rate we open her to traffic in about 1901." "Get the tarp." Verena went back to the springer draft with her own chisel and the light coming long across the gorge. A rider came up the far side on a grey horse, walking it, in no hurry about anything. Merritt picked the order book up off the plank table, carried it down the yard, and set himself on the stack with his back to the road and his shoulders square across the line between the rider and the block she was cutting. He stayed where he was. Verena kept the chisel moving. The mallet came down and the dust came off the arris in a thin steady line and fell across her boot. "Hand me up one of the backing blocks," Merritt said. She stopped, took one off the near stack, and passed it to him. He set it on his knee and did nothing with it at all. "That one wants squaring." "I will square it." The rider went on toward the toll and the dust of him hung in the heat a long while after. Merritt sat with the block on his knee until the road was empty, and then he got up and put it back on the stack. Something had pulled tight across her chest and stayed tight. She set the chisel back on the arris and took the next draft down the face. The crew knocked off at seven. The Ridgely men went down the track together and Emory came past her with his coat over his shoulder. "Same time?" "Half five." He raised a hand without turning round and went. Verena walked the stacks once more in the long light. She dragged the tarp over the fresh course and weighted the corners with rubble. The water bucket went out over the dust in a single throw. Going up the sound stack she counted it by touch as she passed, four, eight, twelve, nineteen, and the far rim had gone the colour of the stone by the time she reached the end of it. The chest sat under its canvas at the edge of the yard. She pulled the block off the corner, threw the tarp back, and carried the three-quarter chisel to the plank table with the small hammer and the drift. The ferrule came off under the drift. Three taps and a quarter turn, three taps and a quarter turn, until the ring walked up the taper and lifted clear. The wedge came out of the head on the fourth tap. She drew the steel out and laid it on the plank, and she was left holding the handle. Ash gone the colour of strong tea. Rubbed to a shine along the underside where four fingers had sat for thirty years. There was a shallow hollow worn at the heel of it, and her father's thumb had made that. Verena fitted her own hand to the wood. Her thumb went nowhere near the place. She stood there a moment with it. Then she sat down on the block behind her and put the handle in her lap. He had made her strike a thousand feet of draft with that chisel before he let her set a single stone into a wall. He had stood at her shoulder in the yard at Bell Rock and told her she was heavy on the tail, heavy on the tail, heavy on the tail, until she stopped being heavy on the tail. Elias Pascoe had been dead since the second week of May, and she had not sat down once. Her shoulders came down. Something went out of her chest with a sound she had not meant to make, and she put the heel of her hand against her mouth, and it came anyway. Verena sat on the block in the empty yard with her father's broken handle in her lap and cried until she was done. "Elias," she said. The name went out across the yard and there was nobody left in it to hear. She sat a while longer with her sleeve across her eyes. The oiled rag was in the bottom tray of the chest where it had always been, black with thirty years of use. She put the wedge and the ferrule inside the split handle, wrapped the wood and the steel together in the rag, and laid the bundle in its own place between the boaster and the drafting chisels. The lid came down. She dropped the block back onto the corner of the tarp and pressed it square with her boot. Her own chisel was still through her belt. She took it out, walked down to the sound stack, and cut a keel stroke and a figure one into the block she meant to set first thing, high on the stack where a man loading in half dark could not miss it. The crew would get tomorrow's order and nothing more than that. Emory would carry it in his face inside a day. The Ridgely men would have it down in Cordell by Sunday, and Sabra would have it out of her at the table in four questions. Corliss would put it into a letter to Tancred inside the week, with that completion clause sitting under her name. Thursday was the next load. It would come up the rim road in the dark off the Ridgely wagons and be down on the skids by half four. She would be standing at the skids when it came. She would take the tally sheet out of the teamster's hand herself and read the name at the bottom of it, and she would watch who rode up behind the wagons and who kept well clear of them. Verena pulled the canvas straight over the sound stack, weighted the last corner, and started down the track toward the road, working out what she would say to the man who signed for Thursday's stone.


----- DRAFT 3 -----

The first eleven blocks rang true. Verena went down the row of skids with the mallet swinging short from her wrist, one strike to a face, listening past the noise of the yard for what came back out of the stone. Clean and high, with the dryness the heat put into everything on the rim by nine in the morning. She had the twelfth counted as sound before she got to it. The twelfth came back dull. The sound shut on itself inside the block and died there. She hit it again in the same place, then a hand's width off, then down on the bed edge, and took the same flat answer three times. She worked the skid bar under the corner and levered the block a quarter turn. The face that came up was dark with quarry damp. She dipped the tin cup in the water bucket and threw the water across it, and the water lay on the surface and ran off the low side without going in. Where it ran, the grain stood open and coarse and the bed lines wandered out of true. Strike, turn, wet the face, read the bed with a nail. Her father had taught that sequence in that order and would not have it done in any other. She got through the whole of it before she noticed she had chosen to. "Emory." He came away from the water barrel with his hat pushed back and his sleeves wet to the elbows. "You've been standing over that one a while." She held out the mallet. "Hit the one behind it. Then hit this." He hit the sound block. He hit the green one. His arm stopped halfway up for a third swing. "That's not the same noise." "No." "What's the difference in it?" "The difference is that one came out of the ground this month." She took the mallet back and hooked it in her belt. "Green stone. It dresses soft, it sets wet, and the first hard frost opens it." Emory looked down the two rows, moving his lips. "How much of it did they send?" "That's what I'm going to find out." She had the order book out of her apron pocket and braced against her thigh before he finished asking. The chalk keel came out with it. She started at the head of the near row and marked as she went, S on the sound faces, B on what would do for backing, W where nothing would do at all, and she wrote each mark into the book with the pencil stub as she made it. "Who brought it up?" "Two wagons yesterday at dusk, three more this morning before you came down." Emory walked the row behind her, keeping out of her light. "Not Corly's teamsters. I never saw the outfit before." "Did they tarp the load?" "There were tarps on it." "Wet or dry underneath?" She wet the pencil and struck out the running tally at the foot of the page. "When you put your hand on the stone in the wagon bed. Was it sweating." Emory took long enough that she looked up. "It was sweating," he said. "I thought that was the weather." "The weather has been dry eleven days." She wrote the tally again, lower. "Who signed?" "I did. The man put the sheet in front of me and I put my name on it. It said Rowe's Quarry, forty-two block, dressed and squared." He rubbed the back of his neck. "It said dressed." "It says a good deal." She stopped at a block with a broken arris and rocked it with her boot to see how it sat on the skid, and it sat badly, high on one corner. She drew the W across it. "This one came off the saw crooked and somebody chased the edge with a hammer to hide it. That's not a quarry's work. That's somebody's work after the quarry." "Rowe's is a straight house." "Rowe's is a straight house." She went on to the next block and struck the keel across it. "Which is a thing worth thinking about." The count came out at forty-two, and the arithmetic came out ugly. Fifteen sound. Nineteen fit for backing and fill. Eight that were not stone she would put into a bridge in any position at all. She underlined the fifteen twice and stood there with the book open on her thigh, and behind the fifteen she set the voussoirs still to cut, the two bearing courses, the springer she had promised the road commission by the fifteenth of September. Every block she had crossed out was a day, and the completion clause did not care whose fault the day was. She wanted a finer chisel to test a joint before she took the loss to anybody. The tool chest sat at the edge of the yard under its tarp with a waste block on the corner to hold it down. She dragged the block off, threw the tarp back, and lifted the lid. The tools lay in the order Elias Pascoe had laid them in, heaviest to lightest, edges to the hinge. She had found them in that order in the trunk in January and had never once rearranged them. She took out the three-quarter chisel and fitted it to her palm to tap a test on the green block, and the head moved. She turned it up. The handle had sprung at the ferrule, a split running up out of the collar for the breadth of two fingers. She tapped it again on the block face and felt the head rock in the socket. Then she carried it out from under the lip of the tarp and held it up into the sun. The wood inside the split was pale and clean. No dirt in it, no oil, no grey. The outside of that handle was the colour of a saddle and had been for twenty years, and the inside had been opened since yesterday, and she had used this chisel yesterday afternoon on the second springer stone and it had been sound in her hand. She went through the chest item by item. Mallets, the two pitchers, the point, the claw, the bull-set, the four-inch, the boaster, the plumb-bob in its cloth. She lifted each one, fitted it, and set it back in its place, and every handle came into her palm worn to a bigger grip than hers, the hollows sitting a half inch wrong under her fingers. "Do you want the sound stone stacked separate?" Emory called from the row. "Separate. Two courses back from the edge, and tarped." She kept her head down over the chest. "Start now, you'll want the morning for it." She laid her father's chisel in its bed, dropped the lid, pulled the tarp over and set the waste block back on the corner. Her own chisel went through her belt. Then she walked out to the middle of the yard where the four of them could hear her. "We're not setting the new delivery today. Bowen, Tervis, you're moving the fifteen marked S up to the abutment platform, and you're moving them like they're the last fifteen in the territory. Emory has the stacking order." "Something wrong with the load?" Bowen said. "Quarry sent a mixed lot. It happens." She was already turning for the platform. "We'll spend the good where it's needed and put the rest where it isn't. Go on." They went on. From the rim road above, the yard would look like any morning in July, men walking stone, a woman with an order book, dust coming up off the skid track. She had been running that performance since May and had got better at it than she liked. Merritt Ballard came up the skid road at two in the afternoon with his coat over his shoulder and the heat pressing down on the whole of the gorge. He stopped at the near row, put his good hand under a marked block, and lifted it clear of the skid. He set it down again slowly. "Where's this out of?" "The sheet says Rowe's." "The sheet's lying." He crouched to the skid, taking the weight off the bad leg, keeping the crushed hand up against his chest and out of the work. He put his thumb along the bed line the same as she had. "Rowe's doesn't sell stone with the sap still in it. What's your count?" "Fifteen sound out of forty-two." He was quiet for a moment. "That's the bearing courses gone." "That's the bearing courses and four days." "Who took the delivery?" "Emory. In the dark, with a sheet in front of him and a lantern." She squatted down across the skid from him. "He signed for dressed and squared." "Did he know the outfit?" "No. And I asked him whether the stone was sweating under the tarp, and it was." She reached behind her, took her father's chisel out of the chest, and held it across the block to him. "And there's this." He turned it over in his good hand, thumbed the split open, and held the ferrule up where the light could get into it. He looked at it a long time for a man who had already stopped moving. "That's not wear," he said. "No." "Somebody's been at your chest." "Yesterday it drove a half inch of granite and never moved." Merritt handed the chisel back. He stayed crouched, looking down the gorge to the falsework standing over the piers, the new timber pale against the old. "Last year they cut the centring and a man died and the whole thing went into the river, and everybody in Cordell agreed it was a bad season and bad luck." "Everybody in Cordell is entitled to their opinion." "And now somebody buys forty-two block of green stone, gets it up a mountain road in the dark, and puts a sheet under the one man on your crew who'd sign it." He got his good hand on the skid rail and came up off the bad leg. "That's not a season." "No." She stood too, and put the chisel back in the chest, and did not put the tarp over it. "It's a man, and he's still here, and he's near enough to walk into this yard at night." "Then we don't lose the week." He was already looking at the stacks. "Show me your fifteen." It took them until five to re-plan it. Sound ashlar held back for the voussoirs and the two bearing courses, nothing green anywhere thrust could get into it. The nineteen went to backing and fill behind the spandrel, dead weight in a place where dead weight was all that was wanted, and the eight went to the rubble pile for hardcore. They chalked as they talked, and the chalk went on faster than either of them was saying whole sentences. "Backing," Merritt said, marking. "That one's better than backing." "You'll want it in the second course." "I'll want it under the springer." She put her own chalk over his. "The springer takes the whole of the arch and I'm not bedding that on a maybe." "Fill, then, this one." "Fill." "You're spending sound stone on a course nobody will ever see." "My father had a rule." She dropped the plumb-line down the face of the abutment and held it, watching the bob steady itself an inch off the ashlar. "Never spend good stone where bad will do. He meant it the other way round as often as not." Emory came past under a block with his shoulders up around his ears. "If the man who sold us this is ever short of work," he said, "I'd like him to know I've got a hole to fill." "Two courses back," Verena said, "and tarped." They set a short course before the light went, to prove the scheme would hold. She spread the mortar bed and struck it off, and Merritt walked the block in on the barrow and helped it down onto the bed with his shoulder into it, and she tapped it home along the line and checked the templet against the springer. It came down true. She set the second and it came down true, and for the first hour since the mallet had gone dull that morning she was doing nothing but the work in front of her. A rider came along the rim road at six, high up and small, moving slow enough to be looking. Merritt was on the platform edge with the barrow. He picked it up, walked it four paces, and set it down on the road side of her, and then he stood where he had put it with his back to the grade and his body between the road and the stone she was dressing. He did not look up at the rim. He picked the four-inch off the plank and started squaring the block at his feet. Verena kept the chisel moving on the arris. The dust came off in a fine line and hung there in the heat before it fell. She could hear him behind her, the mallet going, the shift of his boots on the plank when he changed his stance. "Hand me one off the near stack," he said. She set the chisel down, took a block up off the near stack with both arms, and turned and put it in his hands. It kept them both standing where they were standing. "That's a good face on it," he said. "It'll do." The mallet started again. The rider went on along the rim and out of sight past the shoulder of the hill, and neither of them said anything about it at all. The crew knocked off at seven. Emory went by her with his coat over his arm and his hat already on the back of his head. "There's nothing else in this world I want to lift tomorrow," he said over his shoulder. "Goodnight, ma'am." "Goodnight." She tipped the water bucket out at the edge of the platform and watched the dust take it. She dragged the tarp over the fresh course and weighted the corners, then walked the sound stack a last time, counting by touch as she passed, a hand on each block. Thirteen. The long light lay along the far rim of the gorge and the yard below it had gone into shadow. The chest sat under its tarp at the edge of the yard. She pulled the block off and threw the tarp back and lifted the lid, and took out her father's chisel, and set it on the plank in the last of the light. The ferrule had to come off first. She worked it up off the shoulder of the handle with the flat of a wedge, a quarter turn at a time, until the collar came free and she could lay it down beside the tool. Then she drove the old wedge out of the head with two taps of the small hammer and drew the handle out of the socket. The split ran up from the collar into the palm of the grip. She turned it in her fingers. The wood was worn down into two hollows where a hand had held it for twenty-two years, and her own fingers sat above them and to the outside, and had never once come down into them. She stopped. She sat down on the block behind her with the handle in her lap. She put both palms over it. The gorge was quiet and the light had come off the far rim and gone up into the sky, and in a while her shoulders went down and forward and she let them. "Elias," she said. Her breath went out of her and came back in wrong, and it went on doing that. She stayed on the block. She held the handle against her apron with the split under her thumb and let it come, and nobody came up the skid road, and nothing in the yard needed her. After a time she wiped her face with her sleeve, and reached back into the chest for the oiled rag, and wrapped the handle and the ferrule and the head together in it. She laid the bundle in its own place, edges to the hinge, and set the four-inch back beside it where it belonged. Nothing to the crew. Nothing to Sabra in the letter Friday. The eight waste blocks would go into the hardcore, the loss would go into the schedule where it would sit and cost days, and no one in Cordell would hear a word about a chest that had been opened in the night. She dropped the lid, drew the tarp over, and set the waste block on the corner. There was light enough left to mark tomorrow's block. She took the keel out of her apron and drew the cut lines across the face of the springer stone, the bed, the joint, the arris she would work at seven. Her own chisel went back through her belt. The wagons would come up the skid road at seven, and somebody would climb down with a sheet and want a name on it. She went down to the head of the road, set her boot where she meant to stand, and looked up the grade at the country the wagons would come out of.
