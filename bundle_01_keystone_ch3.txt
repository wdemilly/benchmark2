STEP 2A — ASSESS THREE DRAFTS AND SELECT ONE
(v1 — 2026-07-18. New step. Purpose: the same chapter is drafted three times by Step 2; this step reads all three as literature and selects the one to keep. This is a craft judgment, not a measurement. Derived from a close comparison of three Keystone chapter 2 drafts whose quality differences were large and identifiable; the criteria below are the differences that mattered there. To be run in a fresh incognito window with no other context. Amended 21 July 2026 on Walter's instruction: criteria 3 and 4 are swapped, so dialogue under load is now judged third and information strategy fourth. Reason: on Keystone chapter 10b the old order selected the draft that scored 84 over the draft that scored 91, and the assessment itself named the cause — it ranked the winning draft best on dialogue and then said the criterion order broke the tie against it. Under the new order it selects the 91.)

You are a fiction editor reading three drafts of the same chapter. All three were written from the same outline. Your job is to judge them as prose fiction — as an acquiring editor would — and select the strongest. You are not checking them against the outline for coverage; assume all three cover it.

Read all three drafts completely before writing anything.

Then assess each draft against these criteria, in this order. For every judgment you make, quote the exact passage it rests on. A judgment without a quotation does not count.

1. INTERIORITY. When the chapter goes inside the point-of-view character, is the interior material pinned to specific remembered places, objects, and people, or does it run to summary and abstraction? A memory with a name and a physical detail outranks a reflection that tells the reader what to conclude. Quote the best-pinned interior moment and the most abstract one in each draft.

2. THEME HANDLING. Does any character state the chapter's theme out loud? A speech that announces the meaning of the story is a defect, however well written. Behavior that carries the meaning without naming it is the standard. Quote the most thesis-like line in each draft and the best moment where meaning is carried by action alone.

3. DIALOGUE UNDER LOAD. Find the exchange that carries the most story weight in each draft. Does the dialogue do more than one job at a time — advance the plot, reveal the speaker, and obstruct the other character in the same lines? Quote the single best line of dialogue in each draft and say what jobs it is doing.

4. INFORMATION STRATEGY. What does each draft withhold, and how? The strongest choice lets the reader catch something a character misses, staged in plain sight without narration pointing at it. The weakest explains its own secrets. Identify each draft's central withheld fact and quote how it is planted.

5. MOTIF DISCIPLINE. Does a physical gesture or object recur with accumulated meaning — the same action returning later as metaphor, earned rather than installed? Quote the recurrence if it exists.

6. PRECISION OF THE PHYSICAL WORLD. Are measurements, procedures, and objects exact, consistent, and professionally credible throughout? Note any place where the physical facts blur, contradict, or go generic.

7. LINE FAULTS. Note clumsy constructions, repeated sentence shapes, tense wobbles, and dead phrases, with quotations. Weigh these last; a draft with the best architecture and a few rough lines beats a clean draft with weak architecture.

Then write the verdict:

- Rank the three drafts, first to third.
- For the winner, state in one paragraph why it wins, resting the paragraph on the quoted evidence above.
- For each loser, state in one or two sentences the decisive weakness.
- If two drafts are close, say so and say what would break the tie.
- Name the winner on its own final line, exactly: SELECTED: DRAFT [number].

Do not summarize the plot. Do not suggest revisions. Do not comment on the outline. Judge what is on the page.

----- DRAFT 1 -----

At two o'clock Merritt Ballard unhooked the chain from the post and laid the slack over his bad wrist. The counterweight came down under his good hand a foot of rope at a time, until the crossbar stood level with his shoulder and the pin dropped into its hole. Gravel took the drag of his left boot as he came back around to test it. It held where it was supposed to hold. Nobody in Cordell had ever offered to build him a gate a one-handed man could work, and he had never asked anybody to. The sequence was his. Chain, weight, pin, and then the table under the awning with the tally book squared to its edge. A freighter came down off the Selby grade before the road dust had settled, six mules and a trailer, the driver riding the brake the whole last pitch. "Ballard," the man said. "Four bits for the team. Two for the trailer." "You take silver?" "I take anything that spends." The driver dug in his coat and leaned down with the coins held flat. Merritt put his good palm under them and let the man tip them in, which was the arrangement they had come to a year ago without either of them saying a word about it. "Road's washed out at the second bend," the driver said. "Wagon-deep. You'd want a shovel on it before the freight comes through Thursday." "I'll get down there tomorrow." "You want a hand with that? I could send my boy back." Merritt closed his fist on the coins. "No." The driver looked at him a moment longer than the question needed, and then he looked at the mules, and then he shook out the lines. "Suit yourself, then. Cold for the season." "It'll turn." The trailer went by close enough to throw grit against Merritt's trousers, and the sound of it stayed with him down the whole length of the grade, thinning out over the sage. He waited until the last of it had gone before he went to the table. The tally book opened flat. For four months after the accident he had sat at this table an hour a night with the pencil in the wrong hand, filling the backs of old waybills, and he had kept at it until the letters came out square. He wrote the date at the head of the column, then the team, then the trailer, then the amount. Then the pencil went back into the tin, and the coins went in under the pencil. Six crossings. He ran the column down with the point of his thumb and made it six for the day, with the afternoon most of the way gone. Before the bridge, this road had carried thirty on a slow Tuesday, because there had been forty men working the gorge and everything those forty men ate and drank and wore had come up this grade. There were eleven men in the ground now at the top of the Cordell rise, and the rest of them had gone to Butte or Helena or back east to their wives' people, and the freight that used to come for them came for the town alone, which was to say it came for widows. Six crossings was what a town of widows bought. Merritt shut the book and squared it to the table edge and went out to walk the works. The approach ran two hundred feet from the toll table to the abutment, and the barricade stood across it where the deck stopped. He tested the rope first. The knot was his own, tied one-handed, a thing he retied every day of his life because a knot a man ties with one hand does not stay tied. It gave a half inch and then held. He put his shoulder against the post and it held too. Past the barricade, out where the deck ended, the arch stood open. The springing lines came up true off both abutments, which was the part that people who did not know stone walked right past. Both arms rose toward each other and stopped ten feet short, and the last course of voussoirs sat at the crown on the near side, dressed and numbered, waiting through a winter and a spring for a key that had never been set. Merritt found a plank lifted at the corner and set it back with his boot, then knelt on his good knee and put his weight through his good hand until the nail took. Grit had blown across the abutment stone since the last wind. He swept it off with the flat of his forearm, four passes, corner to corner. A mason had cut that stone, and the man who cut it was in the ground at the top of the rise, and grit lying on his work said that nobody was tending anything. The last ring's stones were stacked under canvas at the head of the approach, numbered in Elias Pascoe's chalk, and the wind had worked the canvas loose at the near corner again. Merritt got his good hand on the rope and hauled it down and put his knee on it while he took a turn around the cleat. Chalk on the top voussoir had held all winter, a figure nine, plain as the day the man wrote it. The falsework scar was below him in the rock. Two rows of sockets cut into the ledge, filled now with a spring's worth of dirt, where the timber had stood that had held the arch up while it was being built. He came back off the approach without going to the edge, and his left hand ached the whole way, and that was all the ache did. Tavish Doorn was at the pump when Merritt got there, which was how the boy generally arrived: already working, so there was nothing to send him home from. The boy had the bucket under the spout and both hands on the handle, and he brought it up and down with his whole body because he did not weigh enough to do it any other way. He was eleven. His boots had belonged to a man. "It's slow today," Tavish said. "It's slow every day." "Six." The boy jerked his chin at the toll table. "I counted from the ridge." "You were on the ridge." "I was up there a while." Merritt took the full bucket in his good hand and let the boy work the handle for the second one. "School's a while, too." "School's done at two." Tavish worked the pump. "Mrs. Teague says I'm to tell you she's got beef on Thursday and you're expected, and she says if you say you're not coming I'm to tell her exactly what you said, in your words, so she can repeat it back to you on Thursday when you come." "You can tell her I'll be there." "That's not what you said last week." "I know what I said last week." The boy stopped pumping and looked at him with the flat patience of somebody who has decided to wait a man out. "You said you'd see how the road was." "The road was fine." "The road's washed out at the second bend." Merritt set the bucket down on the stone. "You were on the ridge and you saw the bend." "I saw the freighter stop and look at it and then not stop again till here." Tavish shrugged and went back to the handle. "That's how you know." Water came up in the second bucket. Merritt let him fill it to the top, which was more than a boy that size should carry, because the boy would carry it either way and would rather be given it than take it. They went up the slope together at Merritt's pace, which was the boy's pace too by then, and he had never once seen Tavish get out ahead and wait. "The agent's here," Tavish said. "The Pascoe one. She was out on the abutment this morning with a plumb-line." "I met her." "Mrs. Teague says she's going to finish it." "Mrs. Teague says a lot of things on a Thursday." "So it's not true, then." "I didn't say that." They set the buckets on the flat stone by the toll table, and Tavish drank out of the dipper with both hands on it and got half of it down his collar. "Mrs. Teague says you could set the last stone yourself if you wanted to. She says you know how." "Mrs. Teague isn't a mason." "She says you were going to be one." Merritt picked a splinter off the flat stone and flicked it away. "A man needs two hands to dress stone. That's not an opinion, that's arithmetic." "You could hold the chisel in the bad one." "No." "You hold the lantern in it." "A lantern doesn't fight back." He looked at the boy until the boy grinned, which took a while, and which was the point of saying it. "Drink your water and quit engineering." The boy sat down on the edge of the flat stone with his elbows on his knees, and he went quiet for a while, which he was not, ordinarily. "Mr. Ballard," he said. "Where was my father standing?" Merritt's hand had gone toward the ledge before the boy finished the question. The mallet was there where it lived, oak-handled, the head worn bright on one face, and his fingers came up under it and closed on nothing at all, because they did not close. He put the hand back down on his thigh. "He was on the centering," Merritt said. "Under the arch, at the north third." "Was he up high?" "He was up high." The boy took that in and turned it over, hunting through it for the part he could get his hands on. "Mrs. Teague says it was quick." "It was quick." "You were there." "I was there." "Then you'd know." "I'd know." Wind came up the gorge and worked at the awning canvas. Merritt kept his ruined hand flat on his thigh and gave the boy the rest of it in the words he had used that morning to the Pascoe agent, and to the coroner's man before her, and to every soul in Cordell who had ever asked. The arch had been loaded before it was keyed. Load had come on wrong. Stone had gone, the centering had gone with it, and eleven men had gone down with the centering. A mason had made an error that a mason does not make. "That's what happened," he said. Tavish nodded and picked at the seam of his boot. "That's what everybody says." "Because that's what happened." The boy nodded again, and let it go, and Merritt stood in the wind with his jaw set and his face doing nothing whatsoever. The light went orange over the Selby foothills and then went off them altogether. "Take that up to Mrs. Teague," Merritt said, and put the smaller bucket in the boy's hands and turned him by the shoulder toward the road. "Tell her it's the good water and not the seep, and tell her Thursday." "You said Thursday already." "Tell her twice." Tavish went up the rise with the bucket held out from his leg, tipping himself the other way to balance it, and he stopped once at the top to shift his grip and did not look back. Merritt watched him out of sight before he went to close the gate. Chain, weight, pin. He hung the lantern on the post and lit it with the wind at his back and the glass braced under his bad wrist. Then he went down to the timber pile. The salvage lay where the crew had stacked it a year ago, forty feet of it, the timbers that had been pulled out of the gorge after and never used for anything since. Merritt set the lantern on a rock, took the top timber by its end, and walked it off the stack one-handed, hauling and setting, hauling and setting, until it lay clear on the grass. He turned it over with his boot and looked at the end of it. He did the same with the second and the third. Standing there, he said her question out loud into the dark, in the words she had used at the abutment that morning, with the wind taking most of it. "How much of the centering was still under the arch when it went?" She had asked it plainly, with no weight on it at all. Then she had waited. He had said something about the coroner's inquest. She had let it go, gone back to her plumb-line, and left the question standing in him the whole day since. Nobody had asked him that. Not the coroner's man, not Corliss, not Halloway, not one of the widows, not once in a year. They had asked him how it fell and how fast and whether their men had known. Every one of those questions his account could answer, because his account began with the arch coming down. Merritt put his palm flat on the ledge where the falsework sockets were cut and worked his thumb into the dirt packed in one of them. The plug came up whole. He rubbed it apart over the rock. Two rows of sockets, timber standing in both of them, holding the arch through the whole build, and he had walked past this ledge four hundred times. What the arch had done, he knew. Where the men had been, he knew, and the sound, and the fact that he had been on the north abutment with the second course of the last ring waiting on the barrow. Somewhere between the barrow and the sound there was a piece of that morning he did not have. A man could tell himself he had been struck, or that a mind takes a thing away for a man's own good, and none of it counted for anything, since he had never once turned and looked at the place where the piece was missing. To answer her, he would have to look at it. His shadow went out long across the timbers. Merritt picked the third one up and set it back on the stack, then took the lantern by the bail and went up the slope with the light swinging ahead of him. "There's a reason I'm still here," Merritt said. He had been saying it for a year. It had gotten him out of the bed in the first month and down the grade in the second, and it had been standing between him and that missing piece ever since, a good plain sentence a man could hold with one hand. A man is left alive for a reason. The reason is the work in front of him. He said it again at the gate and waited for it to shut the thing down. It did not shut it down. Merritt set the lantern on the post. Then he reached up and unhooked the chain, took the pin out of its hole, walked the counterweight down a foot of rope at a time, and raised the bar over a road that nobody would cross before morning. Chain, weight, pin. He worked the sequence through twice more in the dark with his good hand shaking out at the end of it, and by the time he stopped, the thing he had settled was not the question. She would come back down here tomorrow with that plumb-line. He would be standing at this gate when she did.


----- DRAFT 2 -----

The chain came off the post at one o'clock. Merritt Ballard took it in his good hand and walked it back along the rail until the slack ran out, then set his boot to the foot of the counterweight and let the weight down by degrees, riding the drop with his shoulder. His bad leg braced behind him and cut a shallow furrow in the gravel. The ruined hand rode the links above the good one, hooked over them, holding nothing that would be missed if it let go. He pulled the pin from his belt, dropped it through the hasp, and felt it seat. Nine seconds, by a count he kept without meaning to keep it. Two years ago the same sequence had wanted a minute and a half and a man on the far post. A wagon was already on the grade below, coming up slow with the load riding low on the axles. Merritt put his shoulder to the gatepost and waited for it, and by the time Orrin Pike hauled up short of the chain line the toll paper was out of his coat and flat against his thigh. "Ballard." Pike set the brake with his heel. "Road's soft above the switchback. Anything loaded will want the inside track." "I'll tell the next one down." Merritt came around to the near wheel and read the depth of the tire in the gravel. "Sixty over your paper. You're heavier than Tuesday." "Sandstone. Steps for the church, and the church is paying, so take it up with the Almighty." Pike dug in his coat, then stopped digging. "I'll come down after and walk that weight up for you. Save you the business of it at six." "It's set for the afternoon." "It's nothing to me either way." "It's set." Merritt held out the good hand, palm flat, and kept it out until the coins came into it. "They're saying rain Thursday." "They've said rain three Thursdays running." Pike knocked off the brake. The wagon went through and the rear wheel took the rut hard, and he called back over the load without turning his head. "You want anything from town, send the Doorn boy. He's underfoot at your place regardless." Merritt turned the coins once in his palm and went inside. The toll shack held a table, a stool, a shelf and the tin. He set the coins down, squared the tally book to the table edge with the side of his good hand, and opened it to the day. The pencil wanted sharpening before it would take a figure. He wedged it upright in the gap between two table boards, weighted the top of it under the heel of the ruined hand, and cut down toward the wood with the knife, turning the pencil a quarter with his thumb between strokes. Four strokes, four turns. It came up ugly and it wrote. He entered Pike's crossing in the column, the hour and the load and the sixty over. Then he pinned the coins under the lead weight in the tin and shut the lid on them. Fourteen crossings was what the day should have carried. He had four in the book at three in the afternoon and there would be two more before dark, both of them Cordell men going down empty to the mill and coming back the same. Merritt read the column down twice and got the figure he had gotten yesterday and the day before it. He set the figure at the bottom, ruled under it with the flat of the knife, and squared the book to the edge again before he left it. The rounds went in the same order every afternoon. Merritt walked out along the approach with the drag of his boot marking the planks behind him, stopped in the third bay where a plank had lifted in the rain, and worked it down with his heel until the spike took. He put his good hand flat on it and rocked his full weight across it twice, and the plank held under him both times. At the open end the barricade rope hung slack off the post. He tied it back the one-handed way he had worked out in his first month at the gate, the loop pinned against the post with his forearm, the tail fed through with two fingers, the standing part drawn up in his teeth. "Holds," he said to it. It would be slack again tomorrow. Wind came up the gorge every night and worked at the knot loose. He came out and retied it every afternoon of the year. No arrangement he could make with the rope settled the matter for good. The stone ended eight feet past the rope. The arch ran out from this rim and stopped short of itself. Across the gap the crown course sat where it had sat for a year, dressed and numbered, waiting on a key that had never come up the road. Merritt could read the numbers on the near voussoirs from where he stood. He had cut two of them himself, in a shed in Helena, in a year when both hands closed. The ache came up in the second bay, the deep one that ran out of the wrist and into the fingers that no longer made a fist. He swept the grit off the abutment stone with the side of his boot, then went down on the good knee and took the rest of it off with his palm. Sand collected in the joint every day off the road. He cleared it out every day with the edge of his palm. The stone came up gray under his palm with the chisel draft still showing along its edge. The ache climbed while he worked and he finished the stone. Then he came back up the approach with his eyes on the plank line, counting bays, and the count came out at eleven, the same as yesterday. Tavish Doorn was at the pump when he got back, working the handle in both fists with his whole body going up and down after it. The bucket underneath was already half full. The boots the boy stood in had been his father's, and he had grown perhaps an inch into them since November. "You're early," Merritt said. "You're slow." Tavish let the handle rest. "I watched you go out on the deck. You were out there an age." "I was working." "You were standing." He hauled the bucket off the block with two hands and a good deal of leaning. "I've been thinking about the toll." "That's your trouble." "A man walks across, he pays nothing. A man rides, he pays. So what about a man who walks and leads his horse?" "He pays." "That's not fair. That's the same as walking." "The horse would tell you different." Tavish set the bucket down hard enough to slop it over his boots. "The horse isn't paying." "He's crossing." Merritt took the rope end the boy held out to him, and neither of them said anything about the taking, and they ran the coil out along the yard together with Tavish backing away and holding his end high off the dirt. "By your reckoning every man in the county walks his horse over for free and calls it honest." "They'd have to walk the whole way into town after." "They would." "Then I'd charge them again at the top of the road." "There's the toll man in you." "I'd charge double for anything with a dog on it." "Why." "Dogs foul the deck." Tavish said it with great seriousness and went on hauling. "Ask anyone." "Sabra says I'm to be useful somewhere." Tavish tied off his end on the ring, badly, and stood looking at the knot, and Merritt let it stand. "She took me in November. She says the house is quiet enough in the afternoons without me in it." "She's not wrong about the quiet." "She is about the rest of it. She says a boy in the yard is a boy in the way." "You're in the way at the pump. There's water in the trough that wasn't there." The boy carried the bucket to the trough and set it up on the rail for Merritt to tip. That was the arrangement. It had been the arrangement since the first week, with neither of them putting a word to it. Then he went back for the pump handle again. "When do I get out on the span?" "When there's rail on both sides of it." "There's a rope." "A rope tells you where the edge is. It won't do more than tell you." "I know where the edge is." "Then you've no call to go out and look at it." "Jesse Pike went out. He went past the rope and out to the end and back, and nobody stopped him." "Jesse Pike is nineteen and he'll be forty about a week before he's twenty." "He said the stone shakes at the end." "The stone doesn't shake." Merritt set the second bucket down where the boy could get both fists on it. "Wind comes up the gorge and you feel it in your knees and you tell yourself it's the stone. Ask any man who's stood on a scaffold." Tavish worked the handle four strokes while he thought about that. "You're paid to open the gate," he said. "There's nothing in your paper about planks." Merritt let that one go by him and picked up the bucket. The mallet lay on the ledge above the trough where it had lain all spring, the head chipped along one face, the handle gone gray at the grip. Merritt sat down on the ledge beside it to take the weight off the leg. Tavish came back across the yard for the empty bucket, and when he reached it he stopped with his hands at his sides and left it standing in the dirt. "Mr. Ballard. Where was my father standing?" Merritt's hand came off his knee and went out along the ledge toward the mallet. The fingers curled short of the handle and closed on nothing and he brought the hand back down. "Third bay in from the north rim," he said. "On the deck. Not out on the falsework." "Was he working?" "He was setting the block that came down." Tavish stood still with his hands at his sides. The pump dripped twice into the bucket behind him and neither of them moved to shift it. "Was it quick?" "The load came onto that span out of sequence. There was more weight on it than the drawing allowed and the count was wrong somewhere between the yard and the deck." Merritt said it flat, in the order he had said it that morning to the woman from the bonding company, in the same words. "It went in about two seconds. He had no time in there to be afraid." "That's what they said at the inquest." "That's what happened." "All right." Tavish picked up the bucket in both hands. Merritt put his good hand on his knee and pushed himself off the ledge, and it took him two goes. The light was off the yard and up on the far rim by the time he sent the boy home. He fetched the sack from under the table and put it in the boy's arms. "Rhubarb. Take it to Sabra before it goes limp, and tell her it grew on my side of the fence." "It's her rhubarb." "It's my side." "I'll tell her you said that." "Tell her." Merritt stood in the gate and watched him go up the road with the sack held against his chest and the boots kicking out sideways at the bottom of every stride, until the boy was past the second bend. Then he turned to the counterweight. He closed the gate at dusk. The weight came up under his shoulder and the chain went across the road and hooked. He hung the lantern on the post and let the wick settle. His room was twenty feet behind him with a bed in it. He went down the yard path instead, into the timber pile. The salvaged falsework lay stacked where the crew had left it a year ago, the sound sticks squared off in courses, the broken ones thrown in a heap at the low end. Merritt set the lantern on a stump and put his good hand under the top timber and rolled it over toward himself. "'Was the falsework still standing under that arch when the block came down, Mr. Ballard?'" He said it to the timbers in her words, in her flat bonding-company manner, and hearing it out loud did nothing to it. She had asked it once that morning at the gate. She had written down whatever he answered and gone on to the crossing figures, and the question had stayed behind her in the yard all day. The end of the timber in his hand was sawn. He put his thumb along the cut and there was the saw in it, clean across the grain, no split, no tear, nothing of a thing torn out of place under load. He set the timber down on its end and stood it against the stack. The next one was sawn the same, and the third. He stopped turning them and stood with his palm flat on the top of the pile. His account had not changed in a year. Overload on the deck, a bad count, the arch not yet keyed, the block coming down through the third bay. He walked it out once more with the wood under his hand. The account went through end to end without catching on anything. It had never once had to say what was standing underneath that arch. He took the lantern off the stump and carried it thirty feet to the rock at the head of the gorge where the falsework had footed. The scar was cut into the ledge in two rows of pockets, square, a hand deep, dark with a year of rain. Merritt braced the bad leg on the pitch of the ground above them and got down and laid his palm across the nearest pocket. The stone was cold under his palm and had been empty a year. He had no date for the day the crew struck those legs and stacked them. He had never needed one. He held the palm there a while. Then he took his hand off the rock, and the question was standing where it had stood. Merritt came up the path with the lantern swinging low against his knee. The tally book was squared on the table behind him, the figure ruled under it, the coins pinned in the tin under the lead weight. Everything in the yard sat where he had put it, and he had put all of it there himself, and the gate had been shut on time. "I was left standing for a reason," he said. It came out plain and went out over the gorge and did nothing on its way. He said it once more, slower, and got the same result, and set the lantern up on the gatepost where it belonged. Then he took the pin out of the hasp and put his good hand on the chain, and the round began again three hours out of its order, at a gate no rider would cross before morning. The weight came down under his shoulder. His boot cut its furrow in the gravel. The pin dropped and seated. He stood in the lantern light with the chain slack in his fist and made the count anyway. Nine seconds, and nine seconds bought him nothing tonight. She would come back up that road tomorrow with her book and her flat voice and ask him the same question. He shifted his grip on the chain and started the sequence over.


----- DRAFT 3 -----

The chain came off its post at half past noon. Merritt caught the slack across his forearm before the free end could swing into the gate rail, then set his good hand on the crank collar and walked the counterweight down. Three checks on the way down. His right hand held station against the post, the curl of it braced on the wood so the drum could not run away from him. Gravel turned under his left boot. The right boot dragged its half circle through and found the hollow it had worn into the road since March. Merritt dropped the pin in the second hole and let the gate arm stand up against the sky. Ches Wilbanks came up the grade behind six head and a loaded trailer, the dust of them still hanging back at the bend. "Afternoon, Mr. Ballard." "Ches. Six?" "Six and the trailer, and it's heavy." "Dollar forty." Wilbanks came down off the seat with the coins in his fist and started for the tin on the window sill, which was four steps out of his road and always had been. "I'll set it in for you." "Put it in my hand." He put it in Merritt's hand. Merritt closed his fingers on the coins and stood there holding them while Wilbanks looked at the gorge, at the arch standing out over it with nothing on top, at his own boots. "Second bend's washed clear down to the ledge," Wilbanks said. "You'd not want to be walking that." "I'll walk it Thursday." "I could bring a man down Saturday. Wouldn't cost you." "Bring the county a bill. They like paper." Wilbanks laughed once, the sound coming out of him in relief, and climbed back up. "Anything you want said in town?" "That the road's washed." The teams went through at a walk and the trailer took the ruts hard, and Merritt watched the load until it was past the rail and clear. Then he went in. Inside, the toll house held a table, a stove, a shelf, and a window that faced the gate so a man could work and still see the road. Merritt set the coins on the table and opened the tally book to Wednesday. The pencil had gone blunt on Monday. He wedged it under the curl of the right hand, pinned it against the table edge, and cut toward himself with the knife in his left, four strokes and a turn, four strokes and a turn, until the lead stood up clean. Merritt wrote Wilbanks in and the dollar forty beside it and ran the column. Nine crossings, with the afternoon half gone. He put his thumb on the figure and held it there. The book from two years back was on the shelf, and he had no need to open it. Thirty-one crossings for a Wednesday in May, and thirty-eight the Wednesday after, when the freight had been coming up for the works. Nine was what a road got when it went to a gate and the gate went to a hole in the ground. Merritt dropped the coins in the tin and set the flat iron weight on them. He squared the tally book to the table edge with two fingers, took the barricade key off the nail, and went out to walk his rounds. The approach ran two hundred feet from the gate to the open end, planked, with a rope strung across at the last course of stone. The rope was his own work and he retied it every day of the world. He got the knot loose, ran the end around the post and back through, and stood a moment with the bight held down under his knee to keep tension while the good hand did the rest. "Round turn," he said. "Two half hitches." His hand ached from the wrist to the two fingers that were still there, and the ache came up through the work and stayed, and he kept his hand on the rope until the knot was dressed and set. A plank near the rail had lifted at one end. He got his boot on the high edge, rocked it, and pushed the nail down flush with the heel of his palm. Grit had drifted along the abutment stone in a fan. Merritt swept it off with the side of his boot, then crouched, braced on the good leg, and cleaned the bed joint with his fingers until he could see the mortar line. The stone was cut true. He had cut it, on a bench in the yard, in a summer when both hands worked and the whole of it was still only arithmetic and lifting. Four courses up from that seat the arch went out over the Sable in a curve drawn on paper in Helena and made real by nine men and him, and it stopped in air. Its crown course sat on the far side, twelve voussoirs squared and waiting for a key that had never gone in. The falsework scar was in the rock a hundred feet below, two raw notches where the sill timbers had sat. Merritt stood up off the bad leg, put his back to the open end, and started the two hundred feet home along the plank line, counting them off by fours. He counted everything by fours now. It kept the leg honest and the head quiet, and at the hundred and twelfth plank he let the count go and finished on the drag alone. The pump handle started going before he was halfway back, which meant Tavish Doorn had come down the road and let himself into the yard and found something to do with his arms. "You want it full or half?" the boy called. "Full." Tavish worked the handle in a jerk and a haul, his whole body going up on his toes at the top of the stroke. His father's boots came nearly to his knees, stuffed with rag at the toe, and he had been wearing them since October. He got the bucket to the mark, set it down, and stood back from it, breathing. Merritt took the near side by the rim. The boy took the far side without being told, and the two of them carried it in to the stove with nothing passing between them about who took which end, because that had been settled sometime around Christmas and neither of them had brought it up since. "I've been thinking about your toll," Tavish said. "You charge a dollar forty to go up a road that stops." "Road doesn't stop. Road goes to the gate." "Then the gate stops. You know what I mean. A man pays a dollar forty and gets a hole." Merritt worked the stove lid off with the poker and set it back on its ring. "He gets a road he'd otherwise be cutting himself. What you'd like is to come up here free." "I do come up here free." "Then the system's working." The boy went off to the bench, muttering, and came back with the rope end he had been told twice to leave alone. Merritt took it and started the splice, and Tavish kept the standing part pulled tight without being asked, leaning back on his heels with both hands full. The fid went in and the strands came up and the two of them worked it down the lay, the boy paying out an inch at a time whenever he felt the pull come off. "Why can't I go on the span?" "Barricade." "You go past it. You're heavier than me by four stone and you go out there on planks and I can't." Merritt got the splice tucked and pulled it down. He had nothing to say to that, since it was true, and the boy stood there watching him have nothing to say, then came up on his toes again. "Tomorrow you can come out to the rope with me and hold the end while I dress the knot. Not past it. And you wear something on your feet that fits you." "These fit me." "Those fit your father." Tavish looked down at the boots. Then he set his jaw and pulled the standing part tighter, and the two of them worked the rest of the splice out in a quiet that had nothing wrong with it. When it was done the boy coiled the line on the bench in flats, five turns to a flat, and squared the coil off with the heel of his hand, which was Merritt's own habit come back at him out of a nine-year-old. "Sabra says you're to come up for a supper. She said it twice, so you're to come." "Tell her I've a gate." "You had a gate the last four times. That's not a reason. That's a gate." Merritt let that one go by him and let the boy have it, and Tavish took the win without gloating, which was more than most grown men in Cordell could have managed. They carried the second bucket in together. The stove got filled and the coffee got put on. Tavish worked the damper the two-handed way, one hand on the door and one on the rod, and set the kettle where the heat was. He knew where the heat was. Tavish had been coming down at four in the afternoon since the second week of October, when the school let out and there was nobody at his aunt's table who wanted a boy underfoot before supper. "Mr. Ballard." Merritt was at the window ledge, which was where the mallet was, where it had been since a man from the works carried the tools down off the span and set them there and nobody moved them after. "Where was he standing," Tavish said. "My father. When it went." Merritt's hand went out toward the mallet handle. He stopped it a half inch short and the fingers came in on nothing and stayed curled that way against the ledge. "On the crown," he said. "West side, near the third voussoir in." "Was it quick." "It was quick." The boy stood with the bucket down at his feet and both arms hanging. "Folks in town say nobody rightly knows what happened up here." "The arch took a load before it was cured. Somebody read the figures wrong. It came down." Merritt said it flat and level and in the order he had said it that morning to the agent from the bonding company, who had written it into a book of her own. "That's what happened." Tavish took that in and turned it over and put it away somewhere, the same as he did with everything. "All right," he said. The light had gone orange on the far rim by then, which was the hour. Merritt took the parcel down off the shelf, the salt pork he had bought Tuesday and had no use for, and put it in the boy's hands. "Take that up to your aunt." "She'll say she won't have it." "She'll have it. Tell her it's owed on the wash." "You do your own wash." "Tell her anyway, and go on before the light's off the road." Tavish went, the parcel up under one arm, the boots kicking out in front of him at every step. Merritt stood in the yard and watched him take the grade toward town until the boy was a shape against the ruts. Then he turned and went to the gate. The counterweight came up, the chain went over the post, the pin went home in the first hole. Merritt lit the lantern and hung it on its hook. That was the day, closed out in its order. He went down to the timber pile instead. The salvaged falsework was stacked below the works yard where they had dragged it out of the gorge in September, sixty-odd timbers, the ones worth keeping. Merritt set the lantern on a stump and turned the top three over one at a time with the good hand and his shoulder. Twelve by twelve, Douglas fir, sound all through, and the cut ends still bright where the saw had left them. He counted the pile off by fours and got fifty-eight. The bolt holes in the heads were clean and round with the iron still bright in two of them. He got one timber up on end and stood it against his chest, and the head of it came to his collarbone. Timber this size had carried loaded ore wagons for him over the Coeur d'Alene and never once cost him a night's sleep. "If the centering was still standing," he said out loud, to nobody, to the pile, "what did the arch come down on." Those had been her words, and he had them by heart already. She had asked it once that morning, standing on his gravel with her notebook shut, and then she had left it there and gone. Merritt set the timber back down on the flat. The notches at the head were cut true and deep, and he ran his thumb along one and felt the shoulder square under it, and the man who cut that notch had known his trade as well as Merritt knew his own. That arch had not been standing on itself. Not on the twenty-first of April, with the mortar in the crown course four days old, it had not. He went down the bank on the good leg and braced his boot against the pitch of the ground where it fell away, and put his palm flat on the scar in the rock where the sill had sat. The seat was cut level. Somebody had cut it level with a chisel and a wedge and taken the trouble to do it right, and the sill had sat there dead flat and taken the whole weight of the centering, and the centering had taken the arch. That was the order of it. He had raised centering under three arches before this one and struck it in that same order every time. Merritt kept his hand on the rock. The account he had given the county, and the men from Helena, and the agent that morning, and the boy an hour ago, put the load on the arch. The load had not been on the arch. It had been on the timber under his hand, and that timber was in a pile behind him with its ends bright and its notches square, and his story had a hole in it wide enough to walk a team through. He had told it for a year, and nobody had ever once asked him what the arch came down on. Merritt got up the bank, took the lantern off the stump, and went back toward the gate with the light swinging low so he could see his feet. "I was left up here for a reason," he said. He had said it in the hospital in Bozeman and he had said it every week since, and it had always held weight enough to stand on. It went out into the dark over the gorge and nothing came back off it, and the question was still there, exactly where it had been, and his hands knew it before the rest of him agreed. The tally book sat squared to the table edge behind him. The lantern went up on the post. Merritt lifted the pin, took the chain off, and walked the counterweight down in the dark at seven in the evening at a gate no one would cross tonight, three checks on the way down, the pin into the second hole, the arm standing up. She would come up the grade tomorrow with the same question. He set his good hand on the chain and took the weight of it, and he would be standing right here when she came.
