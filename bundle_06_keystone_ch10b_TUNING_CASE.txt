STEP 2A — ASSESS THREE DRAFTS AND SELECT ONE
(v1 — 2026-07-18. New step. Purpose: the same chapter is drafted three times by Step 2; this step reads all three as literature and selects the one to keep. This is a craft judgment, not a measurement. Derived from a close comparison of three Keystone chapter 2 drafts whose quality differences were large and identifiable; the criteria below are the differences that mattered there. To be run in a fresh incognito window with no other context. Amended 21 July 2026 on Walter's instruction: criteria 3 and 4 are swapped, so dialogue under load is now judged third and information strategy fourth. Reason: on Keystone chapter 10b the old order selected the draft that scored 84 over the draft that scored 91, and the assessment itself named the cause — it ranked the winning draft best on dialogue and then said the criterion order broke the tie against it. Under the new order it selects the 91.)

You are a fiction editor reading three drafts of the same chapter. All three were written from the same outline. Your job is to judge them as prose fiction — as an acquiring editor would — and select the strongest. You are not checking them against the outline for coverage; assume all three cover it.

Read all three drafts completely before writing anything.

Then assess each draft against these criteria, in this order. For every judgment you make, quote the exact passage it rests on. A judgment without a quotation does not count.

1. INTERIORITY. When the chapter goes inside the point-of-view character, is the interior material pinned to specific remembered places, objects, and people, or does it run to summary and abstraction? A memory with a name and a physical detail outranks a reflection that tells the reader what to conclude. Quote the best-pinned interior moment and the most abstract one in each draft.

2. THEME HANDLING. Does any character state the chapter's theme out loud? A speech that announces the meaning of the story is a defect, however well written. Behavior that carries the meaning without naming it is the standard. Quote the most thesis-like line in each draft and the best moment where meaning is carried by action alone.

3. DIALOGUE UNDER LOAD. Find the exchange that carries the most story weight in each draft. Does the dialogue do more than one job at a time — advance the plot, reveal the speaker, and obstruct the other character in the same lines? Quote the single best line of dialogue in each draft and say what jobs it is doing.

4. INFORMATION STRATEGY. What does each draft withhold, and how? The strongest choice lets the reader catch something a character misses, staged in plain sight without narration pointing at it. The weakest explains its own secrets. Identify each draft's central withheld fact and quote how it is planted.

5. MOTIF DISCIPLINE. Does a physical gesture or object recur with accumulated meaning — the same action returning later as metaphor, earned rather than installed? Quote the recurrence if it exists.

6. PRECISION OF THE PHYSICAL WORLD. Are measurements, procedures, and objects exact, consistent, and professionally credible throughout? Note any place where the physical facts blur, contradict, or go generic.

7. LINE FAULTS. Note clumsy constructions, repeated sentence shapes, tense wobbles, and dead phrases, with quotations. Weigh these last; a draft with the best architecture and a few rough lines beats a clean draft with weak architecture.

Then write the verdict:

- Rank the three drafts, first to third.
- For the winner, state in one paragraph why it wins, resting the paragraph on the quoted evidence above.
- For each loser, state in one or two sentences the decisive weakness.
- If two drafts are close, say so and say what would break the tie.
- Name the winner on its own final line, exactly: SELECTED: DRAFT [number].

Do not summarize the plot. Do not suggest revisions. Do not comment on the outline. Judge what is on the page.

----- DRAFT 1 -----

"The children want you to come out and see the foal." Cora Jessup stood on the boarding house porch with her chin up and her hands loose at her sides. Her dress had a patch at the left elbow, gray thread worked into brown, the stitches small and even. Lettie had a stack of graded slates against her hip. She set them on the hall table and pulled the door wider. "He came Tuesday," Cora said. "Ruth has told the whole school about him. I expect you have heard." "Four times," Lettie said. "Twice on Thursday, before the bell." "Then you know about his legs." Cora's mouth stayed flat. "It is nine miles out and nine back. I would not ask, only they have got it fixed in their heads that their teacher is coming." "Saturday is mine to spend." "Burr says the road is dry to the crossing, and dry enough after." "Then I will come." It was out of her before she had weighed any part of it. She stood in the doorway hearing it. Behind her Mrs. Kreig poured coffee at the sideboard and set the pot down on its trivet, and the pot was the only thing in the hall that made a sound. Nels came up the street with the team at a walk. The wagon bed rode full, hay roped down in two courses, the rope ends whipped and tucked under. He stopped even with the porch steps and put his weight against the wagon side, and the load barely gave. "Mrs. Jessup," he said. "That is Burr's hay. I have owed him since spring." "You owe him nothing." "We can argue it at your gate." Nels worked the near knot loose a half turn and set it again, tighter. "I am driving out this morning regardless. Miss Pardee, the place sits on my road. You would save yourself nine miles of walking." Lettie had rules about being seen. She had a rule about riding beside a man for nine miles of open country where anyone on the road could count the heads in the seat. "That would be a kindness," she said. Mrs. Kreig came as far as the hall door with the pot still in her hand. She looked at the wagon, then at the two of them, and went back inside. Lettie fetched her satchel and her hat. Nels handed her up over the wheel, and she took the seat rail and kept hold of it. He put his boot to the brake lever and the iron went forward with a clack, and the team leaned into the collars. Roan Creek went past in the order she knew by heart. The mercantile with its crate of onions out front. Kreig's fence and the wash on it. The schoolhouse with its door shut and the bell rope tied off short. Past that the town gave out at a wire fence, and a dog ran the length of it barking. Somewhere past that fence Lettie noticed her hand was down in her lap, off the rail, and she left it there. The road ran out beyond the last wire into sage and climbed by degrees into the foothills. Lupin stood thick in the meadow grass on both sides, blue going purple where the ground held wet. Snow lay in the high seams of the Bighorns to the west. The night's cold was still coming off the sage, and the dust off the wheels came up over it, warm. "There is more of it than last spring," Nels said. "The lupin. Some years you get a mile of it and some years none at all." "The children bring it in wilted. Every May. I have a jar on the windowsill that is mostly stems." "You could tell them to leave it in the field." "I could tell them a great many things." He shifted the lines to one hand and pointed south, out where the ground folded away. "Water under that whole bench. Three feet down, all along it. That is why they filed where they filed." "You sound like you have walked it." "I have walked most of it." He put the hand back on the lines. "It is good country. I did not expect to think so when I came up here." The wagon took a soft place in the road and rode through it heavy. Lettie held the satchel on her knees and worked the buckle with her thumb, the tongue in and out of the hole. "Take them a while," Nels said. "I have never driven a team." "They know the road. You are only holding them." He passed the lines over. The leather was warm from his hands and rougher than she had allowed for. Her palms had been remarked on all winter in Roan Creek, and Mrs. Kreig had said once that a schoolteacher's hands were a wonder to a woman who scrubbed. Lettie set them wide on the lines and let the horses have their heads. "Tell me about the Jessup boy," Nels said. "Burr says he has gone strange over a book." "Danny has gone sane over a book. There is a difference." She watched the ears of the near horse. "He would not read aloud after November. Not one line. In April I put Cooper in his hands, the one about the dog, and he read four pages standing in front of everybody and would not sit down when I told him to." "What did you do about it?" "I let him finish. It cost us arithmetic." "Terrible loss." "It was, in fact. Not one of them can do long division and I have three weeks." Nels laughed, and the near horse put an ear back at the sound. "And the Farrow girl? Still eating by herself on the step?" "Not since the middle of May." Lettie ran her thumb along a mended place in the line. "Ruth Jessup does not ask people things. She sat down next to her with a hard-boiled egg, put half of it on Ellen Farrow's knee, and said nothing at all about it. They have eaten together every day since. Her father has not remarked on it yet." "He will." "He will, and Ellen will tell him she is not moving, and he will find out he has been raising somebody with a spine." A hawk came off a fence post ahead of them and went out over the meadow without hurrying about it. The off horse swung his head to watch it and the lines went slack under Lettie's fingers before she gathered them again. "You hold them too kindly," Nels said. "They have done nothing to deserve otherwise." "Give them an inch on the grade and they will take the whole afternoon." Lettie shortened her hands on the leather and the team came back into the collar. The road bent, and the meadow came open, and she went back over the last few things she had said. They had gone out of her without any weighing at all. Every one of them was true, and none of them was about her. "You will want them slowed at the crossing," Nels said. "How is that done?" "Say so to them. Then sit back." She said so. The team came down to a walk before the water and picked their way over the gravel, and Lettie sat with the lines in her soft hands and a wet wheel turning under her boots. Two miles past the crossing the near wheel dropped into a rut cut deep by runoff. The wagon went over hard, the board seat came up under her, and Lettie caught his arm. Her hand closed above the elbow, over the sleeve. Under it the muscle was set against the pull of the team. Heat came through the cloth into her palm. She let go and got the hand back into her lap. "They cut that one deeper every spring," Nels said. "Yes." The wagon ran on toward the draw. Her hand lay in her lap holding the shape of his forearm, and Lettie left it where it was and looked out at the country coming by. The Jessup place stood in a shallow draw with the corral fence running up the near side of it. Lettie saw the barn from a quarter mile out. Two corner posts and a length of wall plate, standing black, charred down to the grain of the wood. The ground under them had burned to a gray circle with the grass eaten back three feet all around it. Against the corral fence somebody had thrown up a lean-to out of raw boards, and the sawdust was still pale at the foot of it. Nels swung the team and backed the wagon square to the lean-to. He said nothing about the barn. Lettie climbed down over the wheel and crossed the yard to the standing post. She put her hand flat against it. The char was cold, and it went soft under her palm, and it came away on her in a black print from her fingers to her wrist. Sixty feet of bare ground lay between those posts and the house, and forty between the posts and the corral pens, on the one piece of that yard where a fire could burn all night and take nothing else with it. Lettie turned her hand over and looked at the black across her fingers, then closed it into her skirt. The children came around the corner of the house at a run and pulled up short of running. "Miss Pardee." Danny Jessup had his hat off and in both hands. "He is in the paddock. Ruth has the lead, but I get it at the gate." "You do not get it at the gate." "His halter is Pa's old one," Danny said. "It goes round twice." "Then he has room to grow into it," Lettie said. "What is he called?" The two of them looked at each other. "It is not settled," Ruth said. "There are two names and we have been voting." "Then I will hear the case," Lettie said, "after I have seen the animal." She put her clean hand on Ruth's shoulder and let them take her toward the paddock. Behind her the bales came off the wagon into the lean-to, one and another, the new boards knocking each time they took the weight. The foal was all knees. His halter hung with three inches of slack at the cheek strap and Ruth held the lead in both fists. Both children kept their backs square to the burned ground forty feet behind them, and they talked about his feet. Cora came out with a dish towel over her shoulder and stood at the edge of the paddock a minute. "Miss Pardee. Come in out of the sun." The kitchen was dim and smelled of bread. Cora turned a loaf out of its pan onto a board and set the pan down in the wash water. "They came the ninth of April," she said. "Three of them, near one in the morning." Lettie sat down at the table. "Burr went out with the shotgun and they were already at the barn. One of them called him by name. He was not shouting. He said Burr Jessup, and then he said we hear you have not signed." "Had he been asked to sign?" "Twice. A man came in February with a paper and a figure on it, then in March with a smaller figure." Cora laid the flat of her hand on the loaf and turned it over. "They fired the hay first. It went so fast Burr could not get the stock out. Two horses came through the side. The mare did not." She filled two cups from the pot, brought them across, and set one in front of Lettie. Her hand was steady the whole way. "They sat their horses and watched it burn. That is the part I keep. Nobody was in any hurry to go. They stopped at the end of the yard and turned around and watched the roof go, and then one of them said something to the others and they went." "What did the sheriff make of it?" "That there is no telling who a man is in the dark." Cora sat down across from her. "He came out and walked round the posts and wrote in a little book and said he would ask about it. That was nine weeks ago Thursday." "And since?" "Nothing has come since. That is its own kind of waiting." Cora pushed the cooling loaf an inch away from her. "Burr will not sell. He filed on this in eighty-three, he proved up on it, and his mother's chair burned in that barn with everything else. He will not put his name on a paper for any man living. I am not asking him to." "No." "Ruth wakes now when a horse comes up the road. Any horse, any hour. She is nine years old." Cora had both hands around the cup and did not drink from it. "I am afraid all the time, Miss Pardee. In the daylight too. I do not know how much longer afraid is a thing I can carry and still be any use to those children." Lettie put her hand over Cora's hand on the table. Char came off her palm onto Cora's knuckles. The moment for taking it back came and went. She kept hold, and Cora turned her hand over under it and held on. "Wednesday," Lettie said. "Send Danny in with the wagon Wednesday and I will keep them both at the school until you come." Outside, the wagon bed stood empty and the rope lay coiled on the boards. The lean-to was full to the plate. Burr Jessup had come down from the horse pen and stood at the corner of it with his hands in his pockets while Nels set the last bale square. "That is the whole of it," Nels said. "I will send you a colt for it in two years." "You will send me nothing." Burr looked at the stacked hay a while. "Two years," he said. Lettie made herself ordinary for the children and got it nearly right. "Danny. Ruth. Monday, and I want the division done." "We named him," Ruth said. She had the lead wound twice around her hand. "Blue. On account of the field." "Blue is a fine name for a brown horse," Lettie said, and her voice came out steadier at the end of it than it had gone in at the front. They went back into the light off the west with the wagon empty behind them, and the empty bed banged over every rut in the road. The Bighorns went from blue to nothing on the right hand. Lettie put a boot against the footboard on the downgrades and held the satchel buckle in her fingers. "That hay will hold them until the grass comes in on the bench," Nels said. "Will it." "If nothing else happens to them." The wagon dropped its front wheels into the head of the long grade. Lettie set her boot flat and took the drop through her knee. "The crossing will be dark," she said. "There is a moon after eight." Nels drove the last two miles at a walk. Lettie kept the boot on the board and the buckle in her fist, and the quiet in the wagon went the whole distance with the two of them in it. He stopped the team in front of the boarding house, where the windows were already yellow, and came around to hand her down over the wheel. "I will look in on them Wednesday," he said. "Tell me what you find out there." He held the wheel rim a moment longer. "I will." Lettie went up the steps and heard him turn the team in the road behind her. Mrs. Kreig had the lamp lit and a plate kept back under a cloth. Lettie set the satchel on the kitchen table and opened it out flat. The primer came out of it, and the two spare slates, and the pencil stub from the side pocket. Char lay gray across the heel of her palm. She looked at it once and picked up the pencil. She glanced up at the stairs, at the top of them, where her room was and the trunk sat under the window with the key of it in her pocket. Then she pulled a slate over in front of her. "You will want your supper," Mrs. Kreig said. "In a while." Mrs. Kreig moved the lamp nearer the table and stood there a moment. "I will leave the plate by." Lettie wrote Ruth Jessup at the top of the slate and Danny under it, and she began setting down the work she would have ready for them Monday morning, and what she would say to Burr Jessup about letting those two stop the night in town whenever the road was bad.


----- DRAFT 2 -----

"The children want you to come see the foal," Cora Jessup said. "Saturday is the only day I could get in to ask." Lettie had the week's graded slates in both arms. She set them on the hall table and squared the stack straight before she opened the door wider. Cora stood on the step in a gray dress with a patch at the right elbow, a lighter gray, the stitches small and even and set in a neat square. "They have named him, I suppose," Lettie said. "Three times so far. Nothing has held." "Then I had better come and settle it." Cora's hand stayed on the doorframe. "It is eleven miles. I would not ask, only Tom has talked about you at the table every night since the spelling." "Tom has a good ear and a poor opinion of it." "He says you told him so." "I told him the second part." Lettie heard her own voice go out ahead of her, easy, unrehearsed, and she let it stand. "I will come. What time do you eat?" "Whenever there is something to eat by." Behind her, Mrs. Kreig poured coffee at the sideboard and set the pot down on the trivet. She had a way of pouring that filled a room. She said nothing about the door, or the eleven miles, or the woman on the step. Nels Vandergriff came up the road with the hay before Lettie had her bonnet on. He had six bales roped in the wagon bed and he was working the knot at the side rail when she came out. "Jessups," he said. "I promised Burr this in April." "Cora was here." "I passed her on the bridge." He put his shoulder against the wagon side and shoved it, twice, watching the load rather than her. "She said you were coming out. Road runs by their gate." "It runs by a good many gates." "Theirs is the one with hay owed at it." He pulled the rope tail through and made it fast. "Be no trouble to have you up on the seat. Save you the mule." The mule was Mrs. Kreig's and had opinions. Lettie did the arithmetic she had been doing since Kansas. Who would see the wagon go out, who would count the hours until it came back, what a boarding house makes of a schoolteacher on a seat beside a land agent. Mrs. Kreig came out with the pot still in her hand and set it on the porch rail and looked at the hay. "I will get my satchel," Lettie said. She climbed up onto the seat without help from anyone. Nels took the brake lever off, gathered the lines in both hands, and turned the team out through the yard. The wagon creak came up under her and settled into a rhythm she could feel through the board seat. The wheels put dust into the low light behind them. Roan Creek went past in pieces. The mercantile with its shutters folded back, the smithy, three boys on the rail of the stock pen who stopped to watch her go by on a wagon in the middle of a Saturday morning. Lettie held the seat rail through all of it. Somewhere past the last fence she let go of it and put both hands in her lap. The road climbed out of the creek bottom and gave up being a road in any sense a town would recognize. Lupin ran thick in the meadow grass on both sides of it, blue going to purple where the ground stayed wet longest. The cold of the night before came up off the sage for an hour and then the sun took it away from them. The Bighorns stood off on the right hand with snow still lying in the high seams. Lettie got her satchel up onto her knees and started on the buckle, which had been sticking since Kansas. "You will want to work that with your thumb from underneath," Nels said. "I have been working it from above for a year." "How has that gone." "It has gone a year." She turned the satchel over and got her thumb under the tongue and the buckle came free at once. She kept her face where it was. "Say nothing." "I did not say anything." "You were about to." He laughed, and the team's ears came back at the sound of it, and he let them go on. After that he talked about the country, which took him a while and cost him nothing. The meadow they were crossing had been under four feet of water in the spring of '84. A man could still find fence wire caught in the cottonwoods eight feet off the ground if he looked. The lupin was worthless to graze and he liked it anyway. The good ground in this basin, he told her, was rarely the ground anybody fought over. "You sound like a man who means to stay," Lettie said. "I have a room over the land office and a horse I have not named." "That is not what I asked." "No." He shifted the lines to one hand. "I would stay. I did not expect to want to." She turned that over and let it go by without opening it. Her hands were on the leather of the satchel strap, and they were the wrong hands, and she had been told so twice in town in the kindest possible way. She kept them where they were. "Tell me about the boy," Nels said. "Tom." "Tom Jessup could not read in September. He can read now." "How." "Cooper." Lettie found she was smiling and let it happen. "There is a dog in the school yard that belongs to nobody in particular. Tom would not sound a word out for me. He would sound it out for the dog. So I wrote out a page about the dog and I gave it to the dog, formally, in front of everyone, and Tom read it to him because the dog cannot read." "That is either very good teaching or none at all." "I have not decided. The other children now bring the dog their compositions. It has become a great deal of extra correcting." "You correct the dog's mail." "I mark it and I hand it back to the dog." She worked the buckle open and shut, open and shut. "The Terrell girl started eating her lunch with the homesteader children in March. Her father has three thousand acres and she sits on the ground with Tom Jessup and trades a boiled egg for a piece of bread with lard on it, which is the worst trade in the territory, and she makes it every day." "Does her father know." "Her father knows. He asked me about it and I told him she had made a friend and he said, well." Lettie let a wheel take a stone before she went on. "That was the whole of the conversation. Well. Men here can put a great deal into one syllable." "We are economical." "You are avoiding, mostly." "Sometimes both." They went a mile on that. He did not ask her where she had learned to teach, or what her people did, or how a woman came to be alone in Wyoming Territory in her twenties with a satchel from Kansas. She did not ask him what a land agent had done before he was one. The country opened out with the road brown across it. The talk went on and stayed in the present. She got three sentences out of her mouth before she noticed she had not checked any of them first. The noticing came late, and it came with heat in her face, and she made herself finish the sentence she was on. The near wheel dropped into a rut. The board seat came up and threw her hard against him. Her hand went out and closed on his forearm through the sleeve. The muscle came up under her fingers. Heat came through the cloth. She let go before the wagon had finished settling and put the hand back in her lap. "That one has been there since the thaw," Nels said. "I keep meaning to fill it." "You should." He looked at the road. She looked at the road. Her hand lay in her lap with the shape of his arm still in it, and she left it alone. The wagon went on climbing. The mile after that went by in a different quiet from the mile before. The Jessup place sat in a fold of the hills with new wire fence run along the road in front of it. Lettie saw the house first, then the paddock and the well sweep, and then she saw the barn. There was no barn. Four charred uprights stood at the corners of a black rectangle, and the ground inside the rectangle was black too. The grass had come back green in the weeks since and it had come right up to that line and stopped there. A lean-to stood against the house wall with fresh lumber in the frame and nothing in it. Wire and iron lay in the black where the fire had put them down. Nels backed the wagon to the lean-to. Lettie climbed down and crossed to the nearest standing post and put her hand flat against it, high, above the char line where the wood was still wood. Her palm came away with a gray-black print on it. The children came out of the house, Tom first and the two small ones behind him, and all three of them came across the yard at a walk. "Miss Pardee," Tom said. "You came." "I said I would. Where is this animal." "He is in the paddock. He does not like his halter." "Nobody does." The foal's halter was a full-grown horse's halter taken up with twine, and the foal wore it down around his ears at an angle that suggested a hat. The smallest girl had the lead rope in both fists. Tom took hold of the rope above her hands. "Ruthie is too little for the lead." "I am holding it." "You are holding it under me." "Both of you are holding it," Lettie said, "and I am told he has three names, so we will hear them all and then we will vote, and I warn you that I count the votes." She kept her schoolroom face on, the half past two on a Friday face, pleasant and level and giving away nothing. It held. Behind her the bales went off the wagon and into the lean-to, one, two, three. The children walked the foal down the fence line, arguing the whole length of it, and turned him at the corner and argued back. The black rectangle sat in the middle of the yard with the sun full on it, giving off the smell of a wet fire six weeks cold. Cora came out on the step and put her hand up. "Come in out of it. I have the oven going." The kitchen was scrubbed down to the grain of the boards. Cora turned a loaf out of its pan onto a folded cloth, knocked the pan twice against her palm, and set it upside down on the range. She got two cups out of the dresser and talked while she worked. "You will have seen the barn." "Yes." "It was the fourteenth of April. Four men, near midnight, on horses I did not know." Cora set two cups on the table. "They did not knock. They put a rag through the window of the tack room and Burr went out with the shotgun and one of them told him from the dark that he would take the offer while there was an offer." "What offer." "There is a man buys out homesteads on this creek. He has bought four." Cora poured. Her hands were steady and the pouring was even. "Burr told him what he could do. They fired the barn while we stood there and they walked their horses out. Not one of them hurried. That is the part I keep." "Did you go to the sheriff." "Burr rode in the next morning. The sheriff wrote it in a book." Cora sat down across from her. "There is nobody coming, Miss Pardee. I have stopped expecting anybody. My husband will not sell and he will not leave and he is right, and I would not have him any other way, and I am afraid all the time now. I am afraid when the dogs go off at night. I am afraid at four in the afternoon with nothing wrong. I do not know how much longer afraid is a thing I can carry and still be any use to those children." Lettie put her hand over Cora's on the table. She had a year of practice at holding a face still, and she spent some of it now. She held that. She held the hand too, past the point where a hand is patted and given back. Cora let her. The bread went on cooling on its cloth. Outside, Ruthie's voice went up over the paddock rail about the lead rope, and neither woman turned her head, and Lettie did not let go until Cora's fingers moved under hers first. The wagon bed was empty when they came out. Nels had the rope coiled on the boards and the lean-to was full to the joists. Burr Jessup stood at the corner of it with his thumbs in his belt, watching another man stack his hay. "I will square it in the fall," Burr said. "You will square it whenever," Nels said. "Fall." "Fall, then." Lettie went along the paddock fence to say goodbye. Ruthie had the lead now, all of it, and the foal had his head down in the grass and was ignoring her entirely. "Monday," Lettie said. "Tom, bring the reader. Ruthie, bring nothing, you are too little." "I am five." "Then bring five things." She got them laughing, and she went on getting them laughing all the way to the wagon, and holding her voice in that register took more out of her than the eleven miles had. She got up onto the seat and put the satchel on her knees. They went back into a low sun with the empty wagon riding loud over the ruts, every stone in the road coming up through the boards at them. The Bighorns went blue along the top and then went dark on the right hand. Lettie braced her boot against the footboard on the downgrades and worked the satchel buckle with her thumb from underneath, and it opened every time. "Hay held up better than I thought," Nels said. "Was it good hay." "It was last year's." "That is not an answer." "It is the only one I have got." He touched the near horse with the line. "Road is better on the town side. You will want your boot down on the grade after the ford." "I have it down." "So you do." He rode the next four miles without talking. Lettie rode them the same way, with her boot flat on the board and her thumb under the buckle, and somewhere in the second mile she understood that he was carrying the same black rectangle she was carrying. He had been carrying it since he backed the wagon up to it. The quiet went on. It had stopped being distance between them and become a thing they were both sitting inside. He set her down at the boarding house gate in the last of the light. "Monday," he said. "Monday." He turned the team in the road and went on up it, and Lettie let herself in through the kitchen door. Mrs. Kreig had a plate keeping warm at the back of the range, a lamp lit on the table, and the day's dishes done and stacked. Lettie glanced once at the stairs, at the landing, at the door of her room and the trunk behind it with the key in her pocket, and she went to the table instead. She set the satchel down and opened it. She took out the primer and three slates and laid them in a row. The gray-black print was still on her palm, and she looked at it once and picked up her pencil anyway. Tom needed the second reader and there was no second reader in Roan Creek. She could copy out pages. She could copy out pages for all of them, and four of them would be done by Monday morning if she started tonight. It was a small thing. It was inside her reach. "Your supper is getting a crust on it," Mrs. Kreig said. Lettie ruled a margin down the first page and began to write.


----- DRAFT 3 -----

"The children want you to come out and see the foal," Cora Jessup said. Lettie had the graded slates against her hip. She stacked them on the hall table, seven of them squared to the edge, and opened the door wider. Cora stood on the porch boards in a dress patched at the right elbow, the patch a paler blue than the sleeve and stitched small enough to be meant to be seen from a distance and not close up. "He came Tuesday night," Cora said. "Amos has done nothing since but talk about him. He told me you gave the class the horses in Arabia." "I did. Monday, and again Wednesday, because he asked twice." "Then you know my trouble." "I'll come," Lettie said. She heard it go out of her mouth. A year of counting a sentence before she released it, of hearing what a stranger might take from it, and this one had gone past all of that untouched. "Today?" Cora said. "It's four miles and some. I'd not ask it, only he'll be no novelty at all by next Saturday." "Today suits me. I'll want an hour to finish the slates." Cora's hand stayed on the doorframe a moment past the point where a woman lets go of a doorframe. "We'll feed you," she said. "It won't be much." "I'll eat what the children eat." Behind her, Mrs. Kreig poured coffee at the stove and set the pot back on the ring. She looked at Cora, and she looked at Lettie, and she said nothing about any of it. Nels Carrick's wagon came up the street before Cora had got as far as the gate. He drew the team in short of the porch, set the brake, and sat with the lines slack. Eight bales stood roped in the bed with a canvas over the top two. "Mrs. Jessup," he said. "I was bringing this out to you this afternoon. I can as easily bring it this morning." Cora put her hand on the gatepost. "That's your own hay, Mr. Carrick." "It's hay that has been sitting since March getting no better for it." He climbed down and worked the knot at the side rail, took up the slack, and then put his shoulder against the boards and leaned his weight in. The load stayed where it was. "Miss Pardee, your road and mine are the same road today. There's a seat and no trouble in it." Lettie had a rule about being carried anywhere by anybody. She had kept it since October and had never once had to argue herself into it. "I'd be obliged," she said. "Ten o'clock, then." Mrs. Kreig set the coffee pot down on the back of the stove without any comment at all. At ten she came out with her satchel and Nels handed her up onto the board seat. He went round the team, checked the near horse's throatlatch, and climbed up on his own side. The brake lever came back under his hand and the wagon took up its creak. Roan Creek went past them at a walk, the mercantile with its awning still rolled, the bank, Sowerby's with a dog asleep under the rail, and then the last fence and the open road with the dust coming off the wheels behind. Lettie had her hand on the seat rail. Somewhere past the fence she let go of it and put both hands in her lap. The road climbed out of the creek bottom and ran southwest into the foothills, and by the second mile the meadow on either side had gone blue with lupin standing to the wheel hubs. The Bighorns carried snow in the high draws. The morning had been cold and the sage still gave up its smell, and under that was the warmth coming off the dust in the ruts. "You'll want to see the ridge line in about a mile," Nels said. "There's a gap in it that takes the light early." "You sound like a man selling me the parcel." "I've nothing to sell you. I came out here for one season in eighty-four and meant to go back to Nebraska in the fall." "What kept you?" "The grass at Ten Sleep in June comes up to a horse's knee, and nobody had told me. I'd have thought somebody would have written it down." He shifted the lines to one hand. "Do you want these?" She looked at him. "For the flat stretch," he said. "They mostly steer themselves." Lettie took the lines. The leather was warm and stiffer than she expected, and it sat across her palms in a way that would be plain on them by evening. Her hands had been a problem in this town since the day she arrived, soft as a banker's wife's, and Mrs. Kreig had said so twice. "Higher," Nels said. "Let your wrists carry it." "I've held a rein before." "I can see that. It's the sitting straight up that gives you away. You've got a blackboard in front of you and not a team." "That is where I do most of my standing." He laughed, once, and she felt the board seat take it. "Amos says you had them at horses in Arabia," he said. "Amos took a book off my desk in April and read four pages of Cooper standing up, out loud, with everybody watching him. He had been in that class since January telling me he could not read. He can read. He wanted somebody to make him do it in front of Ruby Sowerby." "And did Ruby Sowerby take it well?" "Ruby Sowerby ate her lunch on the Jessup side of the yard on Thursday and again on Friday, which in that schoolyard is a treaty." Lettie brought the near horse back off the crown of the road. "Her father will hear about it by Monday." "Sowerby will live." "Sowerby will send me a note." "Will you answer it?" "I'll answer it with the seating chart," she said. "There is nothing in a seating chart a man can argue with." He took the lines back for the pitch down into the draw, and his hand crossed hers doing it, and neither of them made anything of that. On the far side the road ran flat again through more of the lupin. He named the grasses for her as they went, which ones the cattle took first and which ones came back after a burn, and he did it plainly, without one word of it sounding like a man performing for a woman on a seat beside him. "How many of them are there?" "Grasses? Forty that matter. Nine that decide whether a man keeps his cattle." "Say the nine." He said the nine. She got six of them back at him in order on the second try and missed bluestem twice, and he told her she was the first schoolteacher he had met who wanted to be examined on a Saturday. "I'd rather be examined than talked at," she said. "That's a low opinion of conversation." "It's a fair opinion of most of the conversation offered to a woman in that town." "You've a lot of it by heart," she said. "I've had four years of nothing much to do at supper." "That's a sad account of a country you talk this well about." "It's a fair account of a bachelor's table." He nodded at the ridge. "There. That's the gap." She looked where he told her to look. The light was coming through it and lying down the flank of the near hill in a long stripe, and she watched it until the wagon had carried them past the angle. She said something about the school building's north windows and how they gave her nothing before ten. He said the same thing about his kitchen. Neither of them asked the other where they had learned about kitchens, or windows, or Nebraska, or April. By the time she noticed, three of her sentences had gone out of her without being weighed first. The near front wheel dropped into a rut then, hard, and the seat came up under her and threw her sideways, and her hand went to his forearm and closed on it. There was muscle under the sleeve and the heat of him came straight through the cloth. She let go before the wagon had finished coming out of the hole. "The county calls that a road," Nels said. She put the hand back in her lap. She left it there for the next mile, and it kept the shape it had closed in, and neither of them said one word more about the county or the road or anything else until the Jessup gate. The homestead sat in a bend with its back to a cut bank, and what was left of the barn stood in the middle of the yard. Four uprights, charred black and shining a little in the sun, and the ground inside the footprint was black in a clean rectangle with the grass coming to the edge of it and stopping. A lean-to of new lumber ran off the south side of the house, roofed with tin, empty. Nels backed the team and put the wagon bed square to the lean-to. Lettie climbed down. She put her hand flat on the nearest standing post on her way past it, and the char came off on her palm in a black print of her own fingers. The children were on her before she had straightened up. Amos had the halter and Sarah had the lead rope and the foal had been dragged into the yard between the two of them, three days old, ribby, with a halter meant for a yearling hanging off his nose. "Miss Pardee, he's called Napoleon," Sarah said. "He is not," Amos said. "That's not settled." "Napoleon was short," Lettie said. "This one is going to be tall. You'd be making trouble for him." "He's already tall," Sarah said. "Then hold the lead nearer his chin, or he'll be tall and rude." Lettie showed her the grip and stepped back. "Amos. That halter." "It was Pa's." "Then you'll grow into it together." Sarah walked the foal off toward the paddock with Amos on the other side of him, correcting her, and the two of them going quietly about it, quieter than any two children ought to be with a new horse in the yard. Behind Lettie the first bale went off the wagon and into the lean-to with a sound like a dropped door. Cora came out of the house with her sleeves already turned back. "Come in out of it," she said. "Let them have him." The kitchen was one room with a stove and a table and a curtain across the far corner. Cora turned a loaf out of its tin onto a cloth, knocked the tin twice, and set it upside down on the sill. "They came on the eleventh," she said. "Three of them, near one in the morning. Burr went out with the shotgun and they let him come out. They weren't hiding. That's the thing I keep at." Lettie sat down at the table. "One of them held a lantern up so Burr could see he was being looked at. Then they put the barn up. It went so fast." Cora filled two cups from the pot and brought them over, and her hands were steady doing it. "We had the hay in and the harness and the good saddle. We stood in the door and watched it and there was nothing to do but count it." "Did they say anything?" "They said Burr should think again about the price. That was all that was said in words. Then they sat their horses at the gate until the roof went in, so we'd know they meant to be watched at it." "Did you know them?" "Not their faces. One of them rode a big grey with a walleye and I have seen that horse in Roan Creek, and I have quit saying so out loud." "Say it to me." "I've said it." Cora pushed the loaf a little further along the sill. "It doesn't go anywhere from here. That's what I'm asking." "And Burr?" "Burr won't sell and won't leave and won't say the word sheriff out loud." Cora turned the cups so the handles faced out. "He was forty-one in March. He's got a hundred and sixty acres and a hole in the ground where his barn was, and he'll sit on both of them." Lettie put her hand around the cup and did not drink. "They'll come again," Cora said. "That's not a fear, it's an arrangement. And I have got so I stand at the window at two in the morning to look at my own yard. I'm afraid all day, Miss Pardee. I don't know how much longer afraid is a thing I can carry around and still be any use to those children." Lettie held her face where she held it at the front of a schoolroom. That face had taken her a year to build and it cost her something every day to keep up. "You're of use to them," she said. "That is not in question. The rest of it is not yours to fix." "There's nobody else it belongs to." Lettie reached over and took Cora's hand off the table and held it. She held it. The stove ticked, the loaf steamed on the sill, the children's voices carried in from the paddock, and she went on holding it well after the moment when a hand is ordinarily given back. Cora let her. "Well," she said at last, and got up, and put the cups in the pan. Outside, the wagon bed was empty and the rope lay coiled against the front board. The lean-to stood full to the tin. Burr Jessup had come up from the creek and stood at the corner of it with his hat in his hand while Nels forked the last of it back into the stack. "I'll want to settle for this," Burr said. "You'll want to feed it out first," Nels said. "Then we'll argue." Sarah had the foal's lead still and would not put it down. Lettie made her voice the one she used for a Friday afternoon, easy and a little bored, and said she would want a full report on Napoleon's manners on Monday, in writing, from both of them, and that spelling would count. Amos said something about that not being fair. It came out flatter than it should have. They went out through the gate with the light already down off the ridge and the Bighorns going dark on the right hand. The empty bed was loud over the ruts now, every one of them coming up through the boards. Lettie braced her boot against the footboard on the first downgrade and left it there. "I'll get another eight bales out to them Thursday," Nels said. "Will he take it." "He'll take it Thursday. He wouldn't have taken it Monday." They went on a half mile. She worked the buckle of the satchel on her knees, in and out of the tongue, without opening it. "That's a bad piece of road, that last bend," she said. "It's worse in the wet." "I'd believe it." He put the near horse over off the crown, and she watched him do it. It came to her then that he had been carrying the yard and the black rectangle and the four posts since the moment he backed the wagon to the lean-to. The whole road home he had been driving with it, the same as her. She did not fill the quiet after that, and neither did he, and it was a different thing to sit in than it had been that morning. The lamps were lit along the street when he set her down at the boarding house. "Thank you for the seat," she said. "Thank you for the driving." He handed her the satchel. "Monday." "Monday." He turned the team in the street and went off toward the livery, and Lettie stood on the walk until the wheels quit and then went up the steps and inside. Mrs. Kreig had a plate keeping warm on the back of the stove and a lamp already burning at the kitchen table. Lettie glanced once at the stairs, and at the room up there with the trunk in it and the key in her pocket, and she sat down at the table instead. She opened the satchel. She set out the primer and two clean slates and took up a pencil, and she began a list of what a woman could get into two children's hands by Monday morning without asking anybody in this town for permission. "You've got char on you," Mrs. Kreig said. Lettie looked at her palm, and turned it over, and went on writing.
