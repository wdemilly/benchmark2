STEP 2A — ASSESS THREE DRAFTS AND SELECT ONE
(v1 — 2026-07-18. New step. Purpose: the same chapter is drafted three times by Step 2; this step reads all three as literature and selects the one to keep. This is a craft judgment, not a measurement. Derived from a close comparison of three Keystone chapter 2 drafts whose quality differences were large and identifiable; the criteria below are the differences that mattered there. To be run in a fresh incognito window with no other context. Amended 21 July 2026 on Walter's instruction: criteria 3 and 4 are swapped, so dialogue under load is now judged third and information strategy fourth. Reason: on Keystone chapter 10b the old order selected the draft that scored 84 over the draft that scored 91, and the assessment itself named the cause — it ranked the winning draft best on dialogue and then said the criterion order broke the tie against it. Under the new order it selects the 91.)

You are a fiction editor reading three drafts of the same chapter. All three were written from the same outline. Your job is to judge them as prose fiction — as an acquiring editor would — and select the strongest. You are not checking them against the outline for coverage; assume all three cover it.

Read all three drafts completely before writing anything.

Then assess each draft against these criteria, in this order. For every judgment you make, quote the exact passage it rests on. A judgment without a quotation does not count.

1. INTERIORITY. When the chapter goes inside the point-of-view character, is the interior material pinned to specific remembered places, objects, and people, or does it run to summary and abstraction? A memory with a name and a physical detail outranks a reflection that tells the reader what to conclude. Quote the best-pinned interior moment and the most abstract one in each draft.

2. THEME HANDLING. Does any character state the chapter's theme out loud? A speech that announces the meaning of the story is a defect, however well written. Behavior that carries the meaning without naming it is the standard. Quote the most thesis-like line in each draft and the best moment where meaning is carried by action alone.

3. DIALOGUE UNDER LOAD. Find the exchange that carries the most story weight in each draft. Does the dialogue do more than one job at a time — advance the plot, reveal the speaker, and obstruct the other character in the same lines? Quote the single best line of dialogue in each draft and say what jobs it is doing.

4. INFORMATION STRATEGY. What does each draft withhold, and how? The strongest choice lets the reader catch something a character misses, staged in plain sight without narration pointing at it. The weakest explains its own secrets. Identify each draft's central withheld fact and quote how it is planted.

5. MOTIF DISCIPLINE. Does a physical gesture or object recur with accumulated meaning — the same action returning later as metaphor, earned rather than installed? Quote the recurrence if it exists.

6. PRECISION OF THE PHYSICAL WORLD. Are measurements, procedures, and objects exact, consistent, and professionally credible throughout? Note any place where the physical facts blur, contradict, or go generic.

7. LINE FAULTS. Note clumsy constructions, repeated sentence shapes, tense wobbles, and dead phrases, with quotations. Weigh these last; a draft with the best architecture and a few rough lines beats a clean draft with weak architecture.

Then write the verdict:

- Rank the three drafts, first to third.
- For the winner, state in one paragraph why it wins, resting the paragraph on the quoted evidence above.
- For each loser, state in one or two sentences the decisive weakness.
- If two drafts are close, say so and say what would break the tie.
- Name the winner on its own final line, exactly: SELECTED: DRAFT [number].

Do not summarize the plot. Do not suggest revisions. Do not comment on the outline. Judge what is on the page.

----- DRAFT 1 -----

The land office had one window and it faced east, so the morning came in level across the counter and stood in the dust above it. Merritt Ballard followed Verena through the door and stopped at the hinged gate. Behind the gate a young clerk in sleeve garters was tracing a plat map with a straightedge. He had been at the same map when Merritt passed the window twenty minutes before. "Good morning, Mr. Hollis," Verena said. "We want the filings on the bridge contract. The first season, and the freight records that belong with them." Hollis laid the straightedge down across the map. "Those are office records." "They are public record." "Some of them. There's a procedure for the rest. You'd apply in writing to the territorial surveyor, he would advise this office, and this office would advise you." "How long does that take?" "Six weeks. It has run eight." Verena unbuckled her satchel and laid a folded contract on the counter. She opened it, turned it so the print faced him, and set one finger under a line partway down the second page. "Read that." Hollis read it. He read it again with his lips going, and then he looked down at the signature block, which carried the name of a man in Chicago who had never come west of Bismarck. "That's the contractor's own right of access," he said. "It reaches every filing that touches the work, and copies of them if we want them," Verena said. "I hold his authority." Hollis went into the back room. He came out with three canvas-backed binders and a japanned box of loose freight bills, the lid sprung at one corner, and he set the binders on the counter and looked for somewhere to put the box down. Merritt took it from him. He hooked it up against his ribs with his right arm and let his left hand ride under it for the look of the thing, carrying none of the weight, and he went down the step into the map room with the bad leg dragging on the boards. He did it in front of the clerk. He did it in front of her. He set the box on the long table and opened the first binder. Date, item, weight, hand. He had ruled four years of the same columns at the crossing with a straightedge he cut himself out of a barrel stave. Verena took the chair opposite. Merritt picked his up and carried it around to her side and sat down at her elbow, and neither of them said anything about it. She squared the freight bills into piles by supply house and weighted each pile flat with the brass counter weights. He got the pencil stub out of his coat and turned a used toll slip over to its clean back. "Ninth of May," she said. "Bissell and Sons, Helena. Six hundred running feet, fir, falsework timber." "Who paid the freight?" "It doesn't say on the bill of lading." "It says on the receipted copy. Give me the number off the top." "Four-one-nine." He went through the box with the flat of his crushed hand, pressing the folded bills open one at a time against the table, and the wrist ached under the August heat. He found four-one-nine and read it and wrote Bissell into the first column with the date beside it. "Bissell paid it," he said. "Prepaid at Helena, not billed to the works." "That's generous of them." "It's a bill somebody else got. Next." "Twenty-second of May. Bissell again. Fir, and hardware with it." "Prepaid?" "Prepaid." They worked through the morning. Verena read the dates out and he built the column down the back of the slip, and twice she pushed a bill across before he asked, already ahead of him. The ink on the older dates had faded to the color of weak tea and she held those up to the window to read them. By eleven her piles stood on the table in eight even stacks and one that came to nearly half the height of the rest, and neither of them said why. She had the certificates then, the inspection sheets bound in the second binder, and she went through them with her thumb walking the edge. "These run every fortnight all season," she said. "April. April. May, May, June." Her thumb stopped. "Then the twelfth of July, and then nothing, and then the frame is in the river." "How long is the gap?" "Three weeks." Merritt turned the binder around and tipped it toward the window. Down in the gutter of it, close against the spine, a row of paper stubs stood up under the stitching where the leaves had been cut out with something sharp. He put his thumbnail on the first one. "One," he said. "Two. Three, four. Five." He went on to eleven and stopped. "Eleven leaves out of a bound public book." Verena ran her own thumb along the cut edge and did not take it away again. The last certificate still in the binder carried the inspector's signature at the foot, and the sequence number in the corner of it was 214. The first sheet after the gap was 226. Merritt got up. He went to the counter and put his good hand on it and waited until Hollis looked at him. "Who has drawn these binders before us?" "I couldn't say." "There's a call slip in the front board of each one. You'd have signed them out." Hollis turned his pencil over and set it down. "There's a slip in two of them." "And the third?" "The third has had its slip off since before I came." He said it to the plat map. "I don't keep a name I haven't got, Mr. Ballard." Merritt had not asked a man a question about the span in a year, and he heard his own voice do it steady, and he went back to the table and sat down beside her. He turned the toll slip over to a fresh margin and worked the chain down it in one column while she read him the papers to hang it on. "Freight on the falsework timber," he said, and wrote Bissell and Sons. "Prepaid by the supply house, every load." "Which wants money behind it." "Which wants a surety." He held his hand out and she put the surety schedule into it, and he flattened the fold with the heel of the crushed hand and read down to the block at the bottom. "Bissell's bond is written by Union Indemnity of St. Paul." "That's a name I have seen this morning." "Two pages on." He turned the sheet, and the next, and there it was, a printed assignment with a stamp across the corner and a date under the stamp, the twenty-eighth of March, four months before the falsework went into the water. He put the box lid down across the page for a straightedge and ruled a line under the last name in his column. Then he turned the slip on the table and pushed it across to her. Verena read it down the column slowly, twice. "Bissell hauls the timber. Union Indemnity stands behind Bissell. And Union Indemnity's paper was assigned in March to the Tancred Land and Improvement Company." "Assigned to Tancred's nominee," Merritt said. "Tancred is two lines below that." "A nominee is a coat." She set the slip down flat between them and put the brass weight on it. "Every hand that touched the frame was already being paid, one way around, by the outfit that wanted the town cheap." Merritt folded the slip along its old crease and put it in his inside coat pocket, and he kept his hand on the outside of the coat a moment after. They carried the binders back to the counter at noon and Hollis took them without a word. Verena asked him who in Cordell still held a stake in the bridge company worth the buying. "Whitfield," Merritt said, and Hollis was half a breath behind him with the same name. Outside the heat came up off the street in a sheet and a hitched team stood with their heads down under it. Whitfield's tin sign hung dead still over the boardwalk on the far side. Orlo Whitfield was weighing nails when they came in, and he went on weighing them. He was a big mild man in his shirtsleeves with the cuffs turned back twice, and he poured from the keg scoop into the scale pan in a long rattle and stopped it dead at the weight without looking at the beam. The customer was a boy from one of the river places with a list in his fist. Orlo read the list upside down off the counter and got the boy his lamp wick and his blueing and told him the wick was cut long on purpose and to trim it before he burned it. Merritt looked at the shelves while that went on. There were gaps in the dry goods and the gaps had been dressed, the tins pulled forward to the shelf edge and squared up so the empty behind them would not show from the door. On the counter by the till the account book lay open, and Orlo, between weighing and wrapping, put his hand flat on the left-hand page and slid it down the entries and back up, and did that twice more before the boy had his change. One entry near the foot of that page had been ruled out with a straight pen line. Merritt took a paper of tacks off the shelf and set it on the counter and put a coin down beside it, and the coin bought him a place at the counter and both of Orlo's hands. "Tacks," Orlo said. "There's a man who knows what he wants. Half the trade comes in and stands in the middle of the floor thinking, and I'll tell you what a store hates. Hot, isn't it. My father used to say a Montana August is a Kansas August with the humidity taken out and the honesty left in, and he never was in Kansas." The boy went out. Verena set her hand on the edge of the counter. "Mr. Whitfield, how much of the bridge company do you hold?" Orlo's scoop stopped in the keg. "Enough that it keeps me awake," he said. "Eleven percent," Verena said. "That is what the subscription book shows." "Eleven and a quarter." He set the scoop down in the nails and left it standing there. "It was a fine thing to put money into. You have to understand how it was talked about here in eighty-four. The rails were coming, the span was the reason the rails would come, and every man on this street took a piece of it." "And since?" "The note that let me take my piece has been called." Verena did not move. "By whom?" "The territorial bank at Miles City holds it. Held it." Orlo's hand went to the account book and turned it around to face them, and stopped short of pushing it across. "They sold the paper in the spring. I got a letter saying who to pay, and I have been paying to a box number in Bozeman ever since, and in June they called the whole balance." "The ruled-out line," Merritt said. "That's a payment." "That is a payment I did not make." He looked at Merritt and then away. "Sixty days from the call. That was the twenty-second of June." "Show us the letter," Verena said. Orlo bent under the counter and came up with it and held it out at both edges, upright, so they could read it without taking it. The date at the head was the ninth of July. It offered to buy his interest in the Cordell and Yellowstone Bridge Company entire and to take up his note with it, and the figure was set out twice, in numerals and in words. "That's below what you paid," Verena said. "That was the first one. There have been two since, and the figure has moved." "Which direction?" "Up." Orlo's thumbs pressed the edges of the letter. "It goes up every week the arch stands open and nothing crosses it. The man who wrote that has a table somewhere with the days marked off on it. I am worth more to him on the fifteenth than I was on the eighth." "Who signed it?" Merritt said. "An agent. There's a company behind him with an office in Helena, and I could not tell you who sits in it." He put the letter down on the counter and kept two fingers on it. "I am afraid, Mr. Ballard. I will say that to your faces. I am fifty-one years old and I have got eleven thousand dollars of other people's confidence sunk in a hole in the Yellowstone, and I have a wife, and I am afraid." The scale still held its half weight of nails. Merritt put his good hand flat on the counter beside it. "The span carries on the thirtieth of September," he said. "Hold your paper until the first of October." "And if it does not carry?" "Then you sell on the second of October to the same man for the same money, and you have lost eleven days." "I have lost the store." Orlo said it quietly. "They take the stock. Sixty days from the twenty-second of June is the twenty-first of August, Mr. Ballard. That is five weeks before your thirtieth." "Then we get the five weeks somewhere." "Where?" "I don't know yet." Merritt put his finger on the letter, on the date. "Give me until the twenty-first to find it. Don't answer this before then." Orlo looked at the scale for a while. Then he tipped the pan and ran the nails back into the keg. The light on the counter had gone long and thin, and it climbed the wall and shortened while they stood there, and Orlo did not light the lamp. He brought it up from under the counter and took the chimney off and turned the chimney around in both hands, over and over, and set it down without ever putting a match to the wick. "I want to say a thing and I want you to hear it out," he said. "Cordell is dead. It died the day the survey went north of us, and the bridge is a stone we are all standing on and calling a town. The rails went where they went. They are not coming back for us because we are stubborn." "There's forty families here," Verena said. "There's forty families here with everything they have in lots and stock and a hole in the river, and there is a man willing to pay money for all of it, today, in August, before the winter." Orlo turned the chimney. "There are widows on this street. Mrs. Ansel has her whole husband's savings in the company. If she takes what is offered she goes to her sister in Dubuque with something in her hand. If she waits on the thirtieth and the arch goes in the water again, she goes to her sister with nothing at all and she goes in November. Now which of those is the kindness? I have stood behind this counter three weeks trying to work it out, and every time I come to the end of it I come to the same place. The mercy is the money. The mercy is the money in their hands before winter." Merritt let the last of it sit in the room. "Who told you that?" he said. "Mr. Corliss put it to me that way and I have not been able to get out from under it." Orlo heard himself and stopped. His hand went to the chimney again. "Corliss has been in here." "Twice." Orlo set the chimney down on the counter, upright, beside the unlit lamp. "He does not press. He asks after the store." The room had gone gray enough that the white paper of the letter was the brightest thing on the counter. Merritt said that a man who had already bought the surety on the falsework did not get to call the ruin his own act of charity. Orlo did not answer it. There was no second thing to say. Merritt picked up his paper of tacks. His coin was where he had left it, and Orlo had not touched it. Out on the boardwalk the heat had gone off the boards and the street was blue between the buildings. Merritt took the toll slip out of his coat, unfolded it along its crease, and put it into Verena's hand. "Four names," he said. "It runs one direction the whole way down and it ends at Tancred." Verena held it and read it again in the last of the light. "They will say it is coincidence of trade." "They will say it until we put the dates beside it." Behind them the store lamp came up yellow in the window and laid their shadows out ahead of them on the boards. Merritt took his leg up under him and went off the boardwalk into the street at a pace that made it hurt. "Come on. Your crew is still at the works and they ought to hear the name from us before they hear it from anybody else."


----- DRAFT 2 -----

The land office opened at eight, and the clerk was still working the stove damper when Merritt got the door back with his shoulder. Verena came in past him with her hat in her hand. "We'd like the filings on the bridge company," she said. "And the freight records for the first season of the work." "Those are in the back." "We'll wait while you fetch them." "There's a form for a records request. Two days, ordinarily." The clerk wiped his fingers on a rag and stayed where he was. "It's a busy office." Verena laid the contract on the counter and turned it so the writing faced him. She put one finger under a line partway down the second page. "Read that." He read it through once at arm's length, and then he read it again with his lips moving on the last part. "That is the contractor's entitlement," she said. "You can hand them over this morning, or you can hand them over Thursday with the district man standing where I am." The clerk put the rag down on the stove shelf and lifted the gate in the counter. There were three binders, canvas-backed and heavy at the spine, and a japanned box with the freight bills loose inside it. Merritt took the box off the counter before the clerk could set it down for him. He hooked it up against his ribs and carried it that way, the bad leg dragging on the step down into the front room, and he set it on the long table without asking where she wanted it. He had ruled his own columns in books like these for eleven years. Toll in, toll out, the date at the head of the page, the weather in the margin when the weather mattered. "You'll want the bills," Verena said. "I'll take the dates and the money. You take what came off the wagons." They worked the table from the same side. Verena went through the lading bills two at a time and built her piles by supplier, one at her left elbow and one further out where she had to reach for it. Merritt flattened a folded bill with the heel of his bad hand and set the pencil stub to the back of a toll slip out of his coat. "Sixth of April. Twelve hundred foot of fir, dressed." "Who paid the freight on it?" "It doesn't say here. Say the mill and I'll write the mill." "Write it with a question by it," she said. "The mill wouldn't pay its own freight." He wrote it with a question by it and put the date at the head of the column. She pushed the next bill across without looking up. He had it out of her fingers before it stopped moving. "Twentieth of April, and the freight's paid again at the far end." "Same hand on the endorsement?" "Same hand. Small letters, and he crosses low." "Then he's the one to find." Verena squared a bill on her pile and started another one under it. "Stone next. Anything came up from the quarry, I want the weight and the day." "Weight's on the back of these." "Then read me the backs and I'll keep my hands where they are." He read her the backs of them in the order she had stacked them. She kept two piles going and did not lose either of them. When she wanted a bill again she put her hand out flat without saying which, and he gave her the right one four times running. By the middle of the morning he had brought his chair around to her side of the table, and the japanned box lid sat between them holding down the bills they had done with. The counter weights held the rest flat. The ink on the April dates had gone brown at the edges of the strokes. "Merritt." "Yes." "The inspection certificates run clean to the end of June and then they quit." He turned the binder toward himself. The certificates were bound at the gutter and numbered in a printing at the top corner. The numbers ran on in order, and then they jumped. He put his thumb into the gutter and opened it wider. Along the spine there was a row of paper stubs cut close, each one no wider than a fingernail, the cut edge of them furred where the knife had gone through. "Nine," he said. "Ten. Eleven." Verena leaned in and ran her thumb along the cut edges the whole length of them. "Read me the last one that's still in there." "Certified sound. Falsework members, bents four through nine." He turned it to the light for the signature block and read the name off it, and then he read the date. "Twenty-second of June." The collapse had come on the eleventh of July. He got up and went to the counter with the binder open in both arms, the bad leg loud on the boards, and Verena came with him. "Who's drawn these before us?" "Couldn't say." "You keep a slip for it. There's a slip in the front of every one." The clerk went back to the stove and worked the damper that did not need working. "A man came up from the railhead in the spring. In April, I want to say. He had the whole set out on that table two days running." "His name." "He didn't leave one, and I didn't ask him for one." "You'd have made him write it in the slip," Verena said. "You made me write mine and I've got the contract." "He had business of his own." "Whose." The clerk shut the stove door on a fire that had not caught. "I've told you what I've got. There's a man comes up from the railhead now and then and he looks at what he likes, and I'd as soon keep on being a busy office." Merritt carried the binder back to the table and set it down and did not close it. He worked the toll slip in a column, the pencil down the left side and the money down the right. The freight on the fir was paid at the receiving end by the supply house that shipped it. He wrote the supply house at the head. Under it he wrote the surety that stood on its contract. He went into the second binder for the bond and found it, and two leaves further on he found the assignment of it with a stamp across the corner. "Read me the corner," Verena said. "It's stamped and dated and the date's the sixteenth of March." "Before the timber." "Four months before the timber." He copied the date. "There's a name written into the assignment in a clerk's hand and then the same name printed in the heading." "Say it." "I'd rather you read it off the page yourself in a minute." He set the box lid along the column for a straightedge and ruled a line under the last name in it. Then he turned the slip with one finger and pushed it across to her, and he put the binder beside it open at the assignment. Verena read the column back to him from the top. "Mill to the supply house. Supply house to the bonding outfit." "The other way. The bonding outfit stands behind the supply house." "Stands behind it," she said. She came down the column to the ruled line and stopped, and she put the binder page under it and matched the two. "Tancred." Merritt folded the slip once along the ruled line and put it in his coat with the pencil. "Nine men and a season's work," she said. "And the paper that says it was sound got cut out of a public book in April." "By somebody who came up from the railhead." "By somebody who knew which weeks to take." They carried the binders back through the gate and Verena asked, on the way out, who in Cordell still held any part of the bridge company outright. "Orlo Whitfield," Merritt said. The clerk said the same name half a beat behind him. Outside the heat had come up off the road already, and a team stood hitched in front of the store across the way, under the tin sign with the paint gone off the bottom of it. Orlo was weighing out nails for a woman with a basket, and he did not stop when the bell went. He was a heavy, mild, orderly man with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, and there was an account book open on the counter at his left, and while he poured the nails from the scoop he kept passing his hand down the same page of it. The shelves behind him had gaps in them at the top. Merritt put a coin on the counter and asked for a paper of tacks he had no use for. "Warm again," Orlo said. "It was warm like this the week before last and then it turned. I told Sabra Teague it would turn." "You did." "Tacks, was it. I've got the blued and I've got the common and the blued are dearer by a cent." He poured the woman's nails off into a cone of paper and started the same page of the book over with his hand. "How's the toll bridge treating you, Merritt. I don't see you at this end of the road." "I'm off the toll bridge a while." "So they say. Working stone." Orlo weighed the cone and did not write the sale down. "My father sold nails to the crew that built the first falsework. Eighty-four, that was. I've still got the order in a box somewhere and I'd find it for you if you wanted a laugh." "Mr. Whitfield," Verena said. "You hold a share in the bridge company." The scoop came to rest on the pan of the scale with half its weight of nails still in it. "I do." "How much of one." "The most of what's left in town." He looked at the woman with the basket, and she took her parcel and went out, and the bell went again behind her. "There were eleven of us in it. Now there's me and two widows with a sixteenth apiece." "And somebody's asked to buy you out." Orlo brought a letter up from under the counter. He held it at both edges and let them see the face of it, and he kept hold of it while they read. "That's the third one," he said. "They come about every ten days." "Who signs them." "A clerk in Helena signs them. The name's printed under the fold and it's a company name." "Read it out." "You can see it well enough from there," Orlo said, and he did not turn the letter any further into the light. "The offer improves." "It improves every week the arch isn't closed." He turned the letter and looked at the head of it himself. "That one's the fourth of August. The price on it is better than the last, and the one before that was better than the one before it, and I'd be a liar if I said I hadn't read it twice." Merritt set his finger on the account book, on the entry ruled out in a heavy hand halfway down the page. "That entry's been struck." "It has." "You struck it yourself. That's your hand and you went over it twice." "That's a payment." "That's a payment I owe on the eleventh of September." Orlo laid the letter face down. "My note's been called. It ran three years and it had another eighteen months in it, and the bank has called it, and I've been down there twice to ask what changed. Nothing changed. That's what a man behind a counter tells you when he's been told what to say." "Who holds the paper on it now?" "I asked that too." "Mr. Whitfield." Verena had not moved from the counter. "Say the figure. The whole of what you owe against the whole of what they've offered." Orlo said the figure for the note, and then he said the figure on the letter, and he was some time getting to the end of the second one. "Say the two widows' part as well," Verena said. "A sixteenth apiece. It comes to about what a good team is worth, and they've both had a letter of their own, and Mrs. Sault came in here in July to ask me what I meant to do." Orlo put the scoop down in the pan. "I told her I meant to hold. That was July." "So they've bought your note and they'll buy your share with your own debt," she said. "And the longer the span stands open the cheaper you come." "I'm sixty-one years old." Orlo put both hands flat on the counter, one on either side of the scale. "I've got the store and I've got that share and there isn't a third thing. I'm afraid of it. I'll say that to you plain because you'll hear it in the street anyway. I lie awake with it." Merritt put his good hand flat on the counter next to Orlo's. "Hold till the deadline." "You want a whole season out of me." "I want the arch closed with the town still owning a piece of it. Hold till the deadline and the reversion never comes." "And if she doesn't close it." "Then you sell in November for what they're offering you now," Merritt said. "They'll still want it in November." Orlo turned that over with his hand on the scale, and the half weight of nails sat in the pan through the whole of it. The light on the counter had gone long and thin by then, coming in low under the awning, and the woman with the basket had been the last customer of the day. "You're asking me to carry the town on a note I can't pay," Orlo said. "I'm asking you for eleven weeks." "You're asking me for the store. The two are the same thing and you know they are." He came round the end of the counter and set the shutter bar against the wall where it lived at night. "And I'll tell you what else. You've been off that span a year. A year, and now you stand in my shop and tell me what the town's worth holding." "That's fair." "It isn't fair. It's only true." Orlo stood with his hand on the bar. "You ought to think about those two widows." "I have thought about them." "I don't believe you have. It comes to me at night in a shape you haven't had it in. Cordell is dead, Merritt. The railroad went where it was always going to go and it went there last October. There's forty-one people in this town who'll winter on what they can sell, and money in their hands before the cold is a kindness. Somebody puts money in their hands and they can go to their people in Iowa. That's a mercy and it costs the man doing it a good deal, and I'll tell you he doesn't have to offer it at all." Merritt stood with the paper of tacks in his hand. "You've said all that before." "I've said it to myself before." "You've said it in that order before, and in those words, and it isn't your order." He put the tacks down on the counter and left them there. "Who told you that." Orlo picked the lamp chimney off its bracket and turned it a half turn and set it back without lighting the lamp. "Mr. Corliss put it to me in June." "In those words." "He puts it better than I do." "Elias Pascoe's crew is up in that ground," Merritt said. "Nine of them." He had the second part of it and it would not come. The store had gone dim around the counter by then. The coin was still where he had laid it, and Orlo turned the chimney again in his hands and said nothing at all. Merritt left the tacks where they lay on the counter and went out past the shutter bar. Verena came out after him and pulled the door to behind her. He had the toll slip out of his coat before she was down the step, and he held it out and she took it. "That's the whole of it," he said. "Freight, surety, assignment. It runs at the company that holds the deadline, and the company that holds the deadline has bought Orlo's note." Behind them the lamp came on in the store window and put their two shadows out into the road. "Your crew ought to hear it from somebody who reads paper," Verena said. "Then I'll tell them tonight." He put his hat on and started up the road toward the works. After the first dozen strides the leg came into it, and he went faster.


----- DRAFT 3 -----

The land office smelled of paste and hot dust before nine, and the clerk had not raised the blind over the front window. Merritt came in behind Verena and set his hat on the rail. The bad leg had stiffened on the walk down from the boarding house, and he put a hand on the counter while it came back to him. Verena laid her gloves down. "The filings and the freight records for the bridge contract. First season, through October of eighty-six." "Those are agency records." The clerk buttoned one cuff and started on the other. "There's an application for them. Mr. Ruddock signs it, and Mr. Ruddock is in Helena." "Until when?" "Thursday. Could be the Thursday after." She took a folded contract out of her coat, opened it flat, and turned it so the print faced him. Her finger came down under a line on the second page. "Read that one out." The clerk read it with his lips moving. "Contractor or authorized agent." "Pascoe is the contractor. I hold his authority, and you have seen it twice this month." "I'd be easier with Mr. Ruddock's word on top of the paper." "Put my name in your daybook," Merritt said. "Merritt Ballard, ten past eight, three binders and the freight box. They come back before noon and you can show him the entry." For a moment longer than the sentence needed, the clerk looked at him. Then he lifted the hinged gate and brought out three canvas-backed binders and a japanned box with the bills loose inside it. Merritt took the box up first, hooking it against his ribs where the crushed hand would carry none of the weight, and got it to the table under the window with the bad leg dragging once on the step down. The columns ruled on the binder spines were the columns he had ruled for a year in the toll house. Weight, date, payer, mark. He pulled his chair around to her side of the table and sat where the piles would be under both their hands. "Suppliers with me," Verena said. "Timber apart from the ironwork." "Freight with me." He took the pencil stub out of his coat and squared a blank toll slip in front of him. "What came, when it came, who paid to bring it." She went through the japanned box a bill at a time, snapping each one down onto its heap and putting a counter weight on the heap to hold it. The ink on the older dates had gone brown and thin in the middle strokes. Merritt flattened a folded lading bill with the heel of the crushed hand, held it under the weight until it gave up wanting to close, and read the freight line off the bottom. "Kestler and Sons. Timber, four bills." "Dates," Verena said. "Ninth of May. Twenty-second of May. Fourth of June, and one more in June where the figure has gone soft." "Who paid the freight in?" "Kestler." She turned the next one over without looking up. "Rowe Brothers, ironwork, one bill. Freight paid by the bridge company." "That's how a bill ought to read." He wrote Rowe in the second column and Kestler in the first, and went on writing Kestler. By half past ten her timber heap stood at twice the height of the ironwork, and the freight column on the slip had one name down it eleven times. Verena squared her stack against the table edge with both hands. Neither of them said anything about the two piles. She had the third binder open at the inspection certificates and was turning them by the corner. "Certificates every week from April," she said. "Every week. The last one is the twentieth of July." Merritt put his pencil down. "The falsework stood three weeks past that." "Then the next paper in here is from September, and it is a bill for the removal of the wreck." He turned the binder toward the light and ran his thumb down the gutter. The stubs were there, cut in close to the spine with something sharper than a penknife, so short that the ruled edge showed on only a few of them. "One," he said. "Two, three. Four, five, six. Seven, eight, nine." Verena laid her thumb along the cut edge and held it there. "The numbers run to two hundred and fourteen," Merritt said, "and the next certificate in the book is two twenty-four. The signature block on two fourteen is Halloran, county inspector, and Halloran signs no more of them." He had not put a question to anyone about the span in a year. He put one now, across the room, in front of her. "Who draws these binders?" The clerk had his back to them at the map case. "Nobody draws them. They stay in the case." "Somebody had this one off the shelf with a blade in his hand." "Mr. Ruddock takes the season books into the back office when the company men are down from Helena." The clerk squared a map that was already square. "He takes them in and he brings them out. That's what I've seen, and I'd sooner you had it from him than from me." Verena moved her thumb along the cut edge again, from the top of the gutter to the bottom, and stopped where the paper was whole. Merritt turned his slip to the clean side and blocked out a column down the left of it. "Kestler ships the timber for the falsework," he said, "and Kestler pays the freight on it in." "Which no supply house does for the pleasure of it." "No." He wrote Kestler and Sons at the head. "Bonding on a supply contract sits in the second binder. Find me Kestler." She found it and turned the binder to him with her finger on the line. The bond had been written by Amity Surety of St. Paul, and the signature block was witnessed twice. "Amity," Merritt said, and wrote it under Kestler. "Two pages on." Verena had gone ahead of him. She laid the binder down again with the page open on an assignment, stamped and dated at the head, the ink of the stamp gone half dry so that the second half of the name showed heavier than the first. "Amity signs the whole bond over. Read the assignee." "Tancred Land and Improvement Company." He took the lid off the japanned box, set the edge of it under the last line of his column, and ruled a line across the slip with the pencil laid flat against the tin. "Read the column back to me." "Kestler carries the timber and pays its own freight. Amity bonds Kestler. Amity hands the bond on in September to a company that had no visible business here at all." "August," Merritt said. "The stamp is the fourth of August. A week before the span went into the river." Verena put her finger on the date and read it for herself. Merritt turned the slip around and pushed it across the table to her. Then he picked up the pencil stub and put it back in his coat. They carried the binders back through the hinged gate at noon and stacked them where the clerk pointed. "Who's left in it locally?" Merritt said. "Money in the bridge, living in this town." "There's near nobody left of any size. Whitfield, he's the last one who" "Whitfield," Merritt said, a half beat ahead of him. Outside the heat came up off the street in a sheet, and a team stood hitched and dozing in front of the tin sign that said WHITFIELD, GENERAL MERCHANDISE, CORDELL, M.T. Orlo Whitfield was weighing out nails for a rancher's wife and did not stop when the bell went. He was a heavy man with his sleeves rolled above the elbow. He took the scoop out of the keg, poured into the pan of the counter scale, and watched the beam come up. Two nails went back into the keg between his fingers. The beam settled and he poured the pan off into a sack. Merritt put a dime on the counter and asked for a paper of tacks. He took the near end of the counter and stood there with Verena at his shoulder, a half step back of him. "Tacks I've got," Orlo said. "Tacks and eightpenny nails and about a third of what a man could want." He set the paper down and swept the dime toward himself without picking it up. "You'd be the Pascoe people. Hot enough for you? It's been hot since the Fourth. My brother-in-law says a hot August means an open winter, and he has been wrong about the winter every year since he came out here, so I'd say we're due a bad one on his account." He waited on the joke a moment. Then he opened the account book on the counter, ran his hand down the page and back up it, and wrote nothing. "He's a stockman, is he," Verena said. "Freighter. Was." Orlo turned the page and ran his hand over the new one. There was an entry ruled out with two lines through it near the top, and his palm went across it and came back. The shelves behind him showed daylight at the back on three of them. "Mr. Whitfield," Verena said, "how much of the bridge company do you hold?" The scoop stopped in the keg. "A twelfth," Orlo said. "It was a sixteenth until Dyer sold out and I took his up. That was two years ago, when it was a thing a man wanted more of." "And now?" "Now." He put the scoop down and wiped both palms on the front of his apron. "Now the bank at Miles City has called my note. Ninety days. Forty-one of them are gone." "How much is the note?" Merritt said. "Eleven hundred and forty dollars." "And the store doesn't make that in fifty days." "The store does not make that in a year, with the town buying like it does now." Orlo looked at the ruled-out entry on his own page. "That was a payment on it. I ruled it out because it did not go." Verena leaned her forearms on the counter. "There's an offer for your interest." Orlo reached under the counter and brought up a letter. He held it flat on the wood by both edges and let them look at it without letting go of it. The date at the head was the twelfth of June. "There's been three," he said. "That's the third. Every one has been better than the last." "Better by how much?" Verena said. "Fifty and then fifty. It comes up a little every week the arch is still standing open, and the man who writes it does not pretend otherwise." Merritt read what he could of it upside down. "That's an agent signing. No company on the paper at all." "An agent and a box number in Helena." Orlo's thumbs stayed on the two edges of the letter. "I have written to the box twice. A man answers by return mail every time, inside of four days, which is more than my own bank does." "What does the offer pay?" Verena said. "Two thousand two hundred, as of Tuesday. It clears the note and leaves me the winter." "On a twelfth," Verena said. "Subscriptions went at eighteen thousand for the whole works. A twelfth is fifteen hundred, and you paid Dyer for his on top of that." "Four hundred and forty for Dyer. Nineteen forty in it altogether." Orlo said the figures without hunting for them. "I have done that sum more times this summer than I have done any sum in my life." Merritt set his good hand on the counter and worked it through out loud. "You owe eleven forty on the twenty-second of September. Your stock is thin on three shelves and you can't lay in more until the note is clear, so the store gets worse every week between now and then. The offer clears it, and the offer gets better the longer the arch stands. There isn't a week in there where holding pays you." "There is not," Orlo said. "You have it exactly." "And the others in town are holding because you're holding. Meiklejohn holds a fortieth and he's held it all summer with his eye on you. The day your name goes on that letter, every small stake in Cordell is for sale inside a week." "I know what I am." Orlo let go of the letter with one hand and rubbed the back of his neck. "I'll say it plain to your faces, since you have come in here and asked me. I am afraid. I am fifty-one years old and I have got a wife and my brother's two girls in the house, and I am afraid of this winter, and a man who is afraid signs things." The scale still held a half weight of nails in the pan. Merritt laid his good hand flat on the counter beside the letter, palm down, and left it there. "Hold to the deadline," he said. "That's the whole of what I'm asking you. After it you do as you see fit and I won't come in here about it again." Orlo folded the letter along a crease it already had and put it back under the counter. The rancher's wife took her sack and went out. The bell shook itself quiet behind her, and the light on the counter had come down to a band about a foot wide. Orlo reached the lamp down off its bracket. He took the chimney off and turned it in both hands, and turned it again. "You never saw this town in eighty-three," he said. "Two hundred and ten men on the ore road and my father selling flour off a tailgate. Then the survey came out and the line went south of the divide, and it went where it went, and it is not coming back over the mountain because we would like it to. Cordell is dead. It died the day that survey was printed and we have been paying to keep the body warm ever since." "That's a hard thing to say in your own store," Merritt said. "It's a true thing." Orlo held the chimney up and looked through it at the window and put it down on the counter. "There are eleven widows in this town from the span alone. Eleven. Some of them are holding paper in that company the same as me, and their paper is worth what somebody will pay for it, and there is a man willing to pay for it now. In the spring there won't be. What is the kind thing there? Tell me the kind thing. Money in their hands before the winter is the kind thing, and everything else is a man being sentimental with other people's money." "Who told you that?" "Mr. Corliss put it to me in this store in July, and he was civil about it, and I have not been able to get out from under a word of it since." Orlo stopped. He looked at the lamp on the counter and moved it an inch toward the middle, and the room went on getting darker around it. "The men on that crew have got families in this town too," Merritt said. He waited for the second thing to come and it did not come. He picked his paper of tacks up off the counter and turned it over once. His dime was still lying where he had put it, and Orlo had not rung it up, and the lamp on the counter was still cold. Out on the walk the heat had gone off the boards and the team was gone from the rail. Merritt took the toll slip out of his coat, folded once now, and put it into Verena's hand. "Every line on it runs the same direction. The freight, the bond, the assignment. It's Tancred at the bottom of all three, and it has been since before the span went down." "Then we know the name of the thing we're arguing with," Verena said. "The crew's at the works until dark. They ought to hear it tonight, and they ought to hear it from the man who has kept off that span for a year." The lamp came up yellow in the window behind them, and Merritt went up the middle of the street toward the works with the bad leg picked up into a faster walk than he had taken all summer.
