STEP 2A — ASSESS THREE DRAFTS AND SELECT ONE
(v1 — 2026-07-18. New step. Purpose: the same chapter is drafted three times by Step 2; this step reads all three as literature and selects the one to keep. This is a craft judgment, not a measurement. Derived from a close comparison of three Keystone chapter 2 drafts whose quality differences were large and identifiable; the criteria below are the differences that mattered there. To be run in a fresh incognito window with no other context. Amended 21 July 2026 on Walter's instruction: criteria 3 and 4 are swapped, so dialogue under load is now judged third and information strategy fourth. Reason: on Keystone chapter 10b the old order selected the draft that scored 84 over the draft that scored 91, and the assessment itself named the cause — it ranked the winning draft best on dialogue and then said the criterion order broke the tie against it. Under the new order it selects the 91.)

You are a fiction editor reading three drafts of the same chapter. All three were written from the same outline. Your job is to judge them as prose fiction — as an acquiring editor would — and select the strongest. You are not checking them against the outline for coverage; assume all three cover it.

Read all three drafts completely before writing anything.

Then assess each draft against these criteria, in this order. For every judgment you make, quote the exact passage it rests on. A judgment without a quotation does not count.

1. INTERIORITY. When the chapter goes inside the point-of-view character, is the interior material pinned to specific remembered places, objects, and people, or does it run to summary and abstraction? A memory with a name and a physical detail outranks a reflection that tells the reader what to conclude. Quote the best-pinned interior moment and the most abstract one in each draft.

2. THEME HANDLING. Does any character state the chapter's theme out loud? A speech that announces the meaning of the story is a defect, however well written. Behavior that carries the meaning without naming it is the standard. Quote the most thesis-like line in each draft and the best moment where meaning is carried by action alone.

3. DIALOGUE UNDER LOAD. Find the exchange that carries the most story weight in each draft. Does the dialogue do more than one job at a time — advance the plot, reveal the speaker, and obstruct the other character in the same lines? Quote the single best line of dialogue in each draft and say what jobs it is doing.

4. INFORMATION STRATEGY. What does each draft withhold, and how? The strongest choice lets the reader catch something a character misses, staged in plain sight without narration pointing at it. The weakest explains its own secrets. Identify each draft's central withheld fact and quote how it is planted.

5. MOTIF DISCIPLINE. Does a physical gesture or object recur with accumulated meaning — the same action returning later as metaphor, earned rather than installed? Quote the recurrence if it exists.

6. PRECISION OF THE PHYSICAL WORLD. Are measurements, procedures, and objects exact, consistent, and professionally credible throughout? Note any place where the physical facts blur, contradict, or go generic.

7. LINE FAULTS. Note clumsy constructions, repeated sentence shapes, tense wobbles, and dead phrases, with quotations. Weigh these last; a draft with the best architecture and a few rough lines beats a clean draft with weak architecture.

Then write the verdict:

- Rank the three drafts, first to third.
- For the winner, state in one paragraph why it wins, resting the paragraph on the quoted evidence above.
- For each loser, state in one or two sentences the decisive weakness.
- If two drafts are close, say so and say what would break the tie.
- Name the winner on its own final line, exactly: SELECTED: DRAFT [number].

Do not summarize the plot. Do not suggest revisions. Do not comment on the outline. Judge what is on the page.

----- DRAFT 1 -----

The fourth block in the new load rang wrong under her mallet. Verena struck it again in the same place, an inch in from the arris where the stone had the most of itself to give back. The note came up short and stopped inside the block. She set the mallet on the skid, put her shoulder against the corner and walked the block over onto its bed, and the underside came up with quarry sand still packed in the tooling marks. She tipped the bucket across the face. The water went dark into the stone and stayed dark, spreading wide where it should have stood up and run off. Under the wet the grain showed open and slack, the bed lines wandering away from true. She dragged a thumbnail down the joint. It scored, and left a white line, and a little wet grit came up under the nail. She had gone mallet, water, thumbnail without deciding to. That was her father's order, the same three motions in the same sequence, and she had watched him do it on a hundred loads before she was tall enough to see over a skid. "Emory," she said. "Come and hit this." Emory Sault came across the yard with a pry bar still in his fist and heat shining on his neck. She put the mallet in his hand and pointed at the wet block, then at the one behind him. "That one first. Then the dry one." He struck the wet block. He struck the dry one. He stood there a second with the mallet hanging. "That's not one sound," he said. "No." "So it's cracked through." "It's green," Verena said. "It came out of the ground this spring and nothing has dried it. Set it in a bearing course and it works itself to flour by Christmas." Emory looked at the load, thirty-odd blocks of it on the skids and the wagon boards, and his mouth went sideways. "Well," he said. "That's a fine morning." She took the mallet back off him. The chalk keel was already in her apron pocket, and her order book was in the tool bag under the tarp with her father's plumb-line coiled on top of it. She fetched the book and went down the load block by block. Sound stone got one stroke of the keel on the end grain. Backing got two. Waste got a cross, and the cross went on faster than she wanted it to. She braced the book against her thigh at the end of each skid and worked the pencil stub with her thumb over the point. "Who brought it?" she said. "Teamsters out of Cordell. Not Hollis. Two men I don't know and a boy on the second wagon." "When did it come off?" "First light, near enough. They had it down and the wagons turned before I had the fire going." Emory hauled a block a quarter turn so she could reach the end of it with the keel. "They didn't hang about for the paper, either. Left the bill weighted under a stone on the chest lid." Verena marked a cross and kept the pencil moving. "Whose bill." "Kerrow's yard, it says. Kerrow's mark on the top." "We don't buy off Kerrow." She struck out the running tally at the foot of the page and set the new figure under it. "We have never bought off Kerrow. Elias Pascoe wrote three quarries into that contract and Kerrow is not one of them." "Maybe Kerrow filled in for somebody." "Maybe." She wrote the next figure and struck that one out too. The count came down to eleven sound blocks and four fit for backing, out of a load written up as thirty-four dressed ashlar. She wrote eleven at the bottom of the column and drew a line under it and stood with her thumb over the line. Eleven blocks was two days of setting. The contract wanted the arch closed by the first of October and there was a clause under that date with money hanging off it, and money was not the half of what hung off it. She shut the book. Nothing in the arithmetic would go over into accident. A wrong quarry was accident. A wrong quarry, green stone, bad dressing, and no man left standing by to sign for it made a sentence with somebody's hand in it. She wanted a finer chisel for the wet block, to open the bed and see how deep the green ran. The tool chest sat at the yard's edge under its tarp with a block on the corner to hold it against the rim wind. She threw the tarp back and lifted the lid. The tools lay bedded in the order Elias had left them in, the long chisels along the back, the points and the pitchers in the tray, everything with the handles turned the same way. She took out the second chisel from the left and fitted it and the head moved in her hand. She set the blade on the wet block and tapped it once, light, and felt the shift come back up through the wood. She turned the handle over into the light. The ferrule had ridden up and the wood had split under it, a clean split, running down the grain three inches from the shoulder. The inside of the split was pale. Not one grain of yard dust in it, no oil, no darkening. Yesterday afternoon she had dressed a springer with this chisel and driven it with the two-pound mallet for an hour and it had held sound the whole time. She fitted it again and tapped, and the head rocked. "Do you want the sound stone stacked separate?" Emory called from the skids. "Yes. Stack it now and stay with it." She went through the chest item by item. She lifted each tool, turned it, set it back in its place. Every point and pitcher in the tray sat firm on its tang, and the mallet handles rang solid off the plank. Her father's second chisel, the one with his grip worn into the wood in a place where her own hand had never sat comfortably, had a sprung head and a split full of clean pale wood, and the chest had been under a weighted tarp at the edge of a yard where twelve men worked. She laid the sprung chisel back on its bed and dropped the lid. Her own chisel came off her belt. She drew the tarp over the chest, squared it, and set the block back on the corner. "Right," she said to the yard. "The load's short. We work what's sound and we don't wait on the rest. Emory has eleven blocks stacked at the near end. They go to the abutment in the order he stacks them. The green stone stays where it is and nobody dresses it." Sowerby asked whether they were setting today. "We're setting today," she said. "Get the mortar going." The crew went. From the rim road, from a wagon or a horse up on the grade, it would look like eleven blocks going to an abutment on a hot morning in July, which was what she wanted it to look like. She had been doing this since May and she had got very good at it, and it still cost her something every hour to hold it up. Merritt Ballard came down to the platform after the noon heat had settled in. He walked the stacks first without saying anything to her. At the near end he crouched to the skid, weight off the bad leg, and got his good hand under the corner of a crossed block and lifted it a few inches off the boards and set it down again. "Where's this from," he said. "Kerrow's yard." "We don't buy off Kerrow." "I know it." He turned the block far enough to see the keel cross on the end. "How much of the load." "Eleven sound out of thirty-four. Four for backing. The rest is fill or it's nothing." He was quiet a moment. "And the bill was already made out." "Weighted under a stone on the chest lid before the fire was lit," she said. "Emory never got a name." Verena took the sprung chisel out of her apron and held it across to him. He took it in his good hand, the crushed one held up out of the work against his chest, and he turned the handle to the light the same as she had. "When did you last use it?" "Yesterday. On the springer. An hour of mallet work and it never moved." He looked into the split for a while. "That's fresh wood." "Yes." "Somebody had the tarp off." He gave the chisel back to her handle first. "That's a small piece of work. A man doing that isn't finished." "No," she said. "He isn't." Neither of them put the rest of it into words. Merritt had known she was the mason on this bridge since the third week and had never once said so out loud, and he did not say it now. He set his hand flat on the crossed block. "Then let's spend it where nothing thrusts through it," he said. They re-planned the week on the boards at the end of the platform. "Eleven sound," Verena said. "Six of them want to be voussoirs. That's the springer course and the two above it and no further." "Four to the bearing courses." "Four to bearing. The last one I hold back for the templet." She chalked the numbers on the plank. "Green goes to backing and rubble fill, behind the face, where the load comes down on it flat and nothing works it sideways." "Backing takes weather." "Backing takes weather when it's laid dry. Bed it fat and keep the mortar wet a week and it will hold long enough for what we need of it." "Long enough is doing some work in that sentence." "It's doing all of it," she said. "My father never spent good stone where bad would do. He'd have the green out of the wagon and into a wall before the teamsters were down the grade." They chalked the stacks over, sound at the near end, green run out along the far skids where the light was going first. Emory hauled. Emory said what he thought of Kerrow's yard, of Kerrow's boy, and of the general run of a Tuesday, and then hauled some more. "When a man robs you," Emory said, going past with a backing block on the barrow, "you'd think he'd take the stone away with him, not leave you extra." Verena spread the mortar bed with the trowel and struck it off. Merritt handed the first block up onto the course and she set it, tapped it down to the line, and dropped the plumb-line off the arris. The bob steadied a half inch out. "Off," she said. "Which way." "Toward you. Half." He took the weight on his shoulder and she tapped the block over and dropped the line again and it hung true. They set three more before the light started to go long on the far rim, and the templet went up against the springer and came away right. A rider came up on the rim road while she was dressing the fifth block down to the line. She had the chisel on the arris and the dust coming off in a line under the blade. She heard the horse on the grade, and before she had done anything about it Merritt moved. He shifted a quarter turn on the plank and took his work with him, and he stood between the road and the block she was cutting, and stayed there. She went on cutting. "Hand me that one," he said, nodding at a backing block by her foot. She got her fingers under it and swung it up and he took it against his chest with the good arm. "Set it where?" "Behind you. I'll bed it when you're done with the arris." The horse went along the rim and the dust from it hung and drifted and thinned out. Merritt held the block a good while longer than a man needs to hold a block. Verena kept the mallet going on the head of the chisel, and the line of dust came off the arris steady, and her shoulders knew he was there before she had settled anything about it at all. The crew knocked off at seven with the light still on the top of the far rim. "Night, then," Emory said over his shoulder, going up the track. "Night." Verena walked the stacks a last time and counted the sound blocks by touch as she passed, palm to the end grain, eleven going down to four. She dragged the tarp over the fresh course and weighted the corners. She tipped the bucket out over the boards and the water went in and darkened and vanished, and the yard gave its heat back up at her through the boards. Then there was nobody left on the rim but her, and the chest at the edge of it. She took the block off the corner, drew the tarp back, and lifted the lid. The sprung chisel came out and went on the plank. She set the ferrule on the block edge and worked it off with the pin punch, a quarter turn and a tap, a quarter turn and a tap, until the iron ring came free and rolled a half circle on the plank. The wedge came out next. She tapped it back through the kerf and caught it as it dropped. The handle came off the tang and she turned it over in her fingers. The wood was worn down on the underside where a thumb had ridden it for thirty years, worn into a hollow with a ridge along one edge, and her own thumb sat wrong in it. It had always sat wrong in it. She had gone on using the chisel anyway. She stopped. She sat down on a backing block with the handle in her lap and her wrists on her knees. The split ran pale under her thumb. The oiled rag was still in the tray where he had left it folded in three. "Elias," she said. The sound went out over the gorge and there was nothing on the rim to give it back. She sat there with the wood in her lap and her shoulders came down and her breath went out of her rough, twice, and she let it. She had squared this away in May, packed and bedded and closed, and she had worked eight weeks on top of the lid. After a while she took up the rag and wiped the tang and the ferrule and the split face of the handle. She fitted the wedge and drove it home with two taps of the small mallet and set the ferrule on and rang it down tight, and the head sat firm. She wrapped the chisel in the oiled rag and laid it in its place in the tray with the handle turned to the left, his way, with the others. She dropped the lid, drew the tarp over, and set the weight block back on the corner. Tomorrow's stone was on the near skid with the last of the light still lying along the top of it. She took her own chisel off her belt and the keel out of her apron and marked the bed line and the two joints while she could still see them true, and stood back from it. Nothing would be said. Not to Emory, not to the crew, and not in the Thursday letter to Sabra. The eleven blocks would go into the schedule and the schedule would swallow the loss and nobody up on the rim road would read a thing off this yard but a bridge going up on time. The next load was Friday. Verena put the keel back in her apron, walked out to the head of the track and stood where the grade came over, working out how far down it a wagon showed and how early she would have to be standing there to see who came in with it.


----- DRAFT 2 -----

The mallet came down on the corner of the first block and gave back a sound with no carry in it. Verena Pascoe held the head still against the stone, shifted her stance on the skid, and struck again lower on the face. The same flat answer came up through the handle into her wrist. She set the mallet on the plank and put her shoulder into the block until it rolled a quarter turn. Then she went for the bucket. The dipper of water went across the face and ran, and the grain came up dark and open under the wet, the bed line loose and crumbed where a sound ashlar would have shown tight and close. Her thumbnail dragged the length of that bed and came away with grit packed under it. Quarry-green. Out of the ground inside the month, and dressed by somebody working fast with a dull edge. The chalk keel was out of her apron pocket before she had decided to reach for it. Mallet, then water, then nail, in that order, every time, run so often in the shed behind the Bozeman yard that she went down the list without choosing to. "Emory," she said. She held the mallet out to him handle first. "Strike it where I did." Emory Sault came down off the stack, took the mallet, and hit the block harder than she had. He looked at the head of it, then at her. "That's a dead sound." "Strike the one behind it." He turned and struck the older block on the near stack, and the note came back bright and went on ringing a half second after the blow. He held the mallet still. "That one sings." "Green stone," she said. "It hasn't dried. Set it in a bearing course and it spalls off the face inside two winters." "We ordered two-year seasoned." "We ordered it." She took the mallet back off him. "This is what came off the wagons." The heat stood on the rim already and it was not yet ten. She looked down the row of new blocks laid out along the near edge of the yard, forty-odd of them, and stopped working the delivery and started counting it. The order book came out of the crate under the tarp and she braced it against her thigh to open it, the pencil stub in her teeth while she found the page. Then she walked the row, block by block, drawing the keel across each face as she passed. Sound. Sound. Backing. Waste. She marked a long one twice, waste, and struck the tally at the foot of the column and wrote it again lower down. "Who signed for it?" she said. Emory came along behind her with his hat pushed back. "I did. Six on Tuesday, the rest yesterday off the second team." "Same teamsters both days?" "Different. Tuesday was Halloran's outfit, the ones who bring the lime." She drew the keel across a block with a shake in the corner and wrote waste. "Yesterday I never saw before. Two men, one wagon, no bill of lading on them at all, and they wanted to be gone." "Did they say the quarry?" "They said Pascoe's order, ma'am, and where do you want it." He shrugged. "I put it where you'd see it." She marked another. Waste. "Did you look at the faces when they set it down?" "I looked at the count." He was quiet a moment. "I should have looked at the faces." "You looked at what you were told to look at." She wrote the new figure at the bottom of the column and underlined it. Eleven sound blocks in a delivery of forty-three, where she had counted on forty. Enough to carry the bearing course three days, and the course wanted six. Every waste block was a day, and the completion clause did not care whose fault the day was. She wanted a finer chisel for the sound stone, and the fine work lived in the chest at the yard's edge under the tarp. She threw the tarp back and hooked it over the corner and lifted the lid. The tools lay in the order Elias had left them. She took out the second chisel from the left, the one-inch with the drawn edge, and the handle turned in her fist before she had it clear of the chest. She fitted it back down into the ferrule, seated the head with her palm, and carried it to the nearest block. Two taps. The head rocked. She turned the tool up to the light coming over the rim and looked into the split that ran back from the ferrule an inch and a half along the grain. The wood inside was pale and clean and had no dust in it. She had used this chisel the previous afternoon on the springer, twenty minutes of it, and the handle had been tight. Heat sprung handles. Dry air sprung handles. Neither of them opened a split and left it white as new sap by morning in a yard that ran ankle-deep in stone dust from six o'clock on. She set the chisel on the plank and went through the chest item by item, lifting each one out and laying it in the lid. The mallets were sound. The boaster was sound. The plumb bob and the line came out wound over four fingers and tucked under, his winding, nobody else's. "Do you want the sound stone stacked separate?" Emory called from the row. "Stack it separate and cover it," she said. "Then get the water on." He went. She put the tools back into the chest in his order. She gave the crew the day's revised work in the plainest words she had, standing on the skid where all four of them could hear her, and inside two minutes there were sound blocks going up the plank to the abutment platform. The sprung chisel went into the chest with the split turned down. She dropped the lid, drew the tarp over it, and set a rubble block on the corner to hold it against the afternoon wind. Her own chisel came off the plank and onto her belt. Nobody on the rim road would have seen anything but a yard working. She had been doing this since May, and by now she was very good at it. Merritt Ballard came up from the abutment a little after two with his coat over his shoulder and stopped at the near stack. He put his good hand under one of the marked blocks and lifted it clear of the skid, and she watched him take the weight and then set it back down slower than he had raised it. "When did this come in?" "Yesterday and Tuesday." "You ordered from Hulett's." "I ordered from Hulett's." He crouched to the skid, favouring the leg, and put his knuckles against the wet face where her water had dried out in a pale ring. "This never saw Hulett's bench. Whoever dressed it didn't care whether you took it." "They cared enough to send forty-three blocks and a man in a hurry." She took the sprung chisel out of the chest and brought it over and put it into his good hand. "That was tight yesterday." He turned it over. He held it up and looked down the split with the light behind it, and she saw him find the pale wood inside and understand what the colour meant, and he did not say anything about it at all. "Where does this live?" "In the chest. Under the tarp, twenty feet from the road." "All night." "All night." He gave it back to her. "Then it's the yard, not the wagons." "It could be both." "It could." He got his weight up off the leg using the skid and stood. "How many days does the stone cost you?" "Three, if the backing takes the green. More if it doesn't." "It'll take it." He looked out along the rim road, where the dust hung over the ruts and did not move. "Last year they cut the falsework at Two Medicine and everybody said it was the crew and the crew went home. Nobody went and stood at the site the next morning." "No," she said. "Nobody did." "Somebody should have." She held the chisel against her apron with the split turned in. "I mark my own stone from here on. Every load, before it leaves the wagon." "You'll want a second pair of eyes on the wagons." "I'll have Emory." "You'll have me," Merritt said, and picked up the marked block again and carried it over to the pile she had keeled for backing, and that was the end of it being hers alone. They spent the last three hours of the light making a week out of what was sound. She had the plan on the back page of the order book inside ten minutes. Eleven sound blocks held entire for the voussoirs and the bearing course, nothing green anywhere the thrust ran through it, and the whole green delivery spent on backing and fill behind the face where the load came down dead and no stone worked against another. "Backing takes nine of them," she said. "Ten if I break the long one." "Break the long one. It's shaken at the corner anyway." "Then the fill takes the rest and I want the sound course started tonight." "Tonight." "So I know it beds." She was chalking the sound blocks as she talked, a bar and a numeral on each, and Merritt was reading her chalk over her shoulder and shifting the stacks to match it. "Nine and eleven to the springer. Everything under the bar goes behind." "Templet on nine first." "Templet on nine first." Emory came past under a green block with his arms locked beneath it and his chin up over the top of it. "This is the worst rock I ever carried and I have carried a stove." "Put it behind the face where nobody has to look at it," she said. "That's what they said about me." Merritt dropped the plumb-line off the springer and held it while she spread the mortar bed, and she set the first sound block down into it and tapped it along with the mallet until the arris came true to the line. It went down sweet. It sat where she put it and stayed. Her father had said it a hundred times in the shed, that you never spend good stone where bad will do, and that the man who could not tell the difference had no business owning either. "Elias Pascoe's rule," she said, and got the templet up against the springer to check the splay. Merritt held the other end of the templet. "You knew him." "I worked for him." She read the gap at the top of the templet, a sixteenth, no more. "Bring me twelve." A rider came along the rim road while she had the chisel on the arris of the second block, working the dust off in a long grey line, and Merritt got up off the skid and moved. He did not go anywhere in particular. He carried the templet across to the far side of her, set his shoulders square to the road, and started sorting the green stack from that side instead of the side he had been working. His body stood between the road and her block and the line of dust coming off her chisel. She kept cutting. She was aware of him standing there in a way she had no use for, and the mallet went on tapping down the arris at the same rate it had been going. "Hand me one of the shaken ones," he said. She set the chisel down, picked up a green block, and put it into his arms, and he took his time about turning to receive it, which kept him standing where he was standing. "That's the last shaken one." "Then give me a sound one and I'll give it back." The rider went on along the rim without slowing, and the dust rose behind him and hung in the heat and came apart. She picked up her chisel and put the edge back on the arris. The crew knocked off at seven with the light still good on the far rim. Emory went by with his coat over his arm. "Same time," he said. "Earlier." He raised a hand without turning round and went down the trail towards the road. She walked the stacks once more before she covered them. The sound stack she counted by touch, going along the row with her fingers on the faces, four courses of two and one on the top, and the count came out the same as her book. She dragged the tarp over the fresh course and weighted the four corners with rubble, and tipped the water bucket out over the mortar board. The yard went quiet at seven every evening, and she was the only one left in it. She threw the tarp off the chest and opened it and took out her father's chisel and sat down on the plank with it across her knees. The ferrule wanted to come off. She worked it round with the pliers, a quarter turn and a quarter turn back, easing it up the taper, and it came free and she laid it beside her. Then the wedge. She set the point of a nail against it and tapped it out with the small mallet, three light strikes, and it dropped into her palm, a scrap of oak no bigger than a thumbnail with sixteen years of driving in it. The handle came apart along the split and she turned the two pieces over in her fingers. The hollow was there. Halfway down, on the underside where a right hand closed, the wood was worn away into a shallow dish that her own grip did not fill and never had. She had held this handle a thousand times and her hand had always sat forward of that hollow, because the hollow was his. She stopped. She sat on the block with the pieces of the handle in her lap and did not do anything else with them for a long while. Her thumb stayed in the hollow. Out beyond the rim the light went along the far wall of the gorge and dropped down it, and the swallows came out over the works and turned and went back. "Elias," she said. The chest was open at her feet. His mallets lay in it head to head where he had laid them, his boaster beside them, the plumb line wound and tucked under since before she could reach the bench. She had not changed one thing in that chest since the day it came off the train, and she had told herself it was because his order was a good order. She got the oiled rag out from under the lid, wrapped the ferrule and the wedge into the two halves of the handle, and bound the whole of it round twice. She laid it in the chest in the place where it went, second from the left. Then she wiped her face with the back of her wrist and got up off the block. The lid came down. She drew the tarp over the chest, squared it at the corners, and set the rubble block on it. Nothing about the yard looked different from the road. She was not going to tell Sabra. She was not going to tell the crew, or write it into the report that went east on Fridays, or put one line of it in the book. The three days would go into the schedule where the schedule was thickest and come out the other side buried, and if the deadline held, nobody would ever know the stone had been bad. She took her own chisel off her belt and went to the block she had laid out for morning, the long sound one for the second voussoir, and knelt to it while the light was still on the face. The keel went across it in a single bar with the numeral under it and an arrow for the bed. The next delivery was Thursday. She stood, put the chalk away, and went down the trail towards town working out how early she would have to be in the yard on Thursday to be standing at the wagons when they came in, and who she would be watching when they set the tailgate down.


----- DRAFT 3 -----

The seventh block came up dead under the mallet. Verena struck it again, square on the face, and got back the same flat knock with no ring anywhere in it. She set her boot against the skid and rolled the block a quarter turn. Along the bed side the drafting margin ran ragged where a claw chisel had been driven at the wrong angle. She tipped the bucket and threw a cup of water across the face. The stone took it in patches. Dark spread quick through the middle and stalled wet at the edges, and the grain stood up coarse and open under the shine. Her thumbnail caught on the bed line and left a pale scratch behind it. Mallet, water, nail. Her father had run a delivery in that order for thirty years, and she had gone through all three before she thought about doing it. "Emory." She straightened up off the skid. "Come down here and bring your ears." Emory Sault came off the top of the stack in two steps. She held the mallet out to him. "Hit that one. Then hit the one behind it." He struck. He struck again, and his head came around. "That's two different noises." "It is." "The near one's soft somewhere in the middle of it." "It's green. Cut this spring, never sat a winter." She took the mallet back and hung it through her belt. "There's water still in the bed. It will carry weight all summer and open up the first hard frost after we are paid and gone." "It looks sound to me." "So it will, till November." She crouched and ran her thumb down the ragged margin. "And look what they did to the dressing. That is a man being paid by the block." Emory whistled through his teeth. "The whole load?" "That is what we are about to find out." She took the chalk keel out of her apron pocket and started at the head of the row. Sound stone got an S at the shoulder, backing a B, waste a W struck through twice so nobody could argue it back later. Her order book came open against her thigh, the pencil stub worn so short she held it against the ball of her thumb. "Who brought it up?" she said. "Two wagons. Tuesday, near dark." She marked a B and moved along. "Whose teams?" "Not Kearns's." Emory came after her down the row, stepping over the skids. "I would know Kearns's mules from the bottom of the grade." "Then whose?" "Fellow named Doss on the first wagon, and I never saw him before in my life. He said Kearns was behind on the top beds and had put the order out to keep us moving." The pencil stopped. She looked up the row at nineteen blocks she had not yet marked and did the sum she did not want. "Did he say to whom?" "He said it was all one stone in the end." "It is not all one stone." She wrote the W and drew the second line through it hard enough to break the pencil lead. "Who signed the sheet?" "I did. You were up at the abutment with the templet and I was not going to send two loaded wagons back down the grade at dark." "You did right." She went on down the row. "Did he leave a bill?" "He left a sheet of paper with a figure on it." "Bring it to me before you go tonight." Twenty-nine blocks came off those wagons and eleven of them took an S. At the tail of the row she braced the book on her thigh, struck out the week's tally, and wrote it again underneath, lower. Under that she put the number of days and drew a line beneath it. Four days out of a completion clause that had eleven left in it this morning, and eleven had been thin. The marking wanted a finer edge. At the yard's edge the tool chest sat under its tarp, and she threw the canvas back off the lid and knelt to it. The tools lay bedded in the order Elias Pascoe had left them, coarse to fine, heads to the hinge. She lifted out the inch chisel, and the handle turned in her grip before she had it clear of the box. She fitted the head and tapped the chisel twice on the face of the block. The handle rocked at the ferrule and came back crooked. Up in the light she turned it slowly. The split ran three inches down the ash from the ferrule, and the wood inside it was pale and clean and dry. There was no dust in the crack and no grit worked down into the grain. She had cut the springer bed with that chisel yesterday and driven it an hour with a two-pound mallet. It went across her knee. Ash goes at the ferrule when the wood is old and the winter has been dry. Four handles had opened in her father's shop over the years, every one along the grain with the season's dirt already worked down into the split. This one she turned over again. She put her nail into the split and brought it out clean. Item by item she went through the chest, lifting each tool onto the tarp and setting it back. Nothing was gone and nothing else had been handled. "Do you want the sound stone stacked separate?" Emory called from across the yard. "Yes. Start it now." She laid her father's chisel in its bed with the split turned down. Then she took her own inch chisel from the row below it, dropped the lid, and pulled the tarp over and weighted the corner with a waste block. The crew stood at the water barrel watching her walk the row. She came back across the yard with her own chisel through her belt and gave them the day in plain language. "Eleven blocks off that delivery go to the abutment and nowhere else. The rest is backing, and nobody sets a marked block in a bearing course. Willem, take two men and start the sound stone moving up." "And the ones with the W?" Willem said. "They are still stone. They will do for fill behind the wall where nothing comes through them." They went. From the rim road above, a man looking down would have seen an ordinary yard at work in late July. That was the whole of what she wanted him to see, and she had got very good at it since May. Merritt Ballard came up the plank walk to the abutment platform in the middle of the afternoon with his coat over his arm, and he stopped at the skids where the marked blocks stood. He crouched to the near one with his weight carried off the left leg and got his good hand under the corner. He lifted it a hand's breadth, held it, set it down square. "Who signed for this?" "Emory. Tuesday, at dark." "This never came out of Kearns's ground." "No. Kearns put the order out to somebody else, or that is the story Doss gave." "How much of it is any good?" "Eleven out of twenty-nine." He put his knuckles against the wet patch on the face of the block, where the water had still not gone off. "And the clause?" "Four days, if nothing else happens between here and September." He stood up slowly, taking his time about the leg. "Four days is not the worst of it. You could work green stone if it came in honest and you knew what you had. This came in dressed to look right." "That is what I have been standing here thinking for two hours." "A man cutting corners sends you rough stone cheap. He does not pay somebody to dress the margins on rubble." He looked down the row at the S marks and the B marks and the struck-through W. "Somebody wanted it up on this bridge." She took the sprung chisel out of her apron and put it into his hand. He turned it over. His crushed hand stayed against his hip and out of the work, and he got the ferrule up to the light with his thumb along the split, the same turn she had given it herself. "When did this go?" "I drove it an hour yesterday afternoon." He looked into the split for a while. "It is clean in there." "It is." "Then he was in this yard between yesterday's whistle and this morning." He handed it back to her, and he did not put anything on top of the sentence. She stood with the chisel in both hands and found that her shoulders had come down off her ears for the first time since the mallet. She put it back in her apron and picked up the keel. "Then we had better make the week out of eleven blocks," she said. They took the stacks apart and built them again. Sound ashlar came out to the near skids under a chalk mark for the voussoirs and the two bearing courses under them. The green went to the far side for backing and fill, where the load runs down through the mass and nothing thrusts out through the face. "Springer course wants your best four," Merritt said. "It wants five. The fifth carries the templet." "Then the fourth backing course is short." "The fourth backing course can be rubble and lime and I will not lose a night over it." She chalked a five on the block at her knee. "Never spend good stone where bad will do. My father used to say it about twice a day and I always thought he was being cheap." "He was being cheap." "He was being right." She stood and pointed with the keel. "That one and that one. Bearing course." They set a short run before the light went, to prove it out. Merritt spread the mortar bed and she came behind him with the block. It went down and she tapped it home to the line with the heel of the mallet. The plumb-line dropped off the springer and hung dead still in the heat. She held the templet against the curve and read the joint at the top and the bottom of it. "Two hairs proud at the back." "Take it up," he said. "I am taking it up." Emory came past under a backing block with his arms straight down and his neck gone red. "When the bridge falls in the river," he said, "I want it read out that I signed for the stone at night and in the dark." "You will be the first man named," Merritt said. They set it three times before the joint came right, and the light on the far rim had gone the colour of a new penny when she let it stand. A rider came along the rim road above the works, walking his horse, and the dust came up off the road behind him in a low brown line. Merritt was crouched at the skid to her left. He got up, moved four feet across the platform, and set himself down again on the other side of her with his back to the road and his shoulders square to it. He picked up the same block he had put down. She kept the chisel moving along the arris. The dust came off the edge in a fine grey line and the mallet strokes went on at the same count. "Hand me that block," he said. She set the chisel down, took it up off the skid with both hands, and put it across into his good hand. He turned it and looked at the bed. "This one is better than I thought." "It is off the top of the load. Doss put the good stone where a man would look first." "Then keep it for the springer." "I will." He stayed where he was until the horse had gone around the shoulder of the gorge and the dust had settled back into the road. Then he set the block down on the skid a foot from her knee and left it there. The crew knocked off at the whistle from the works below. Emory came by with his shirt over his shoulder. "Night, Miss Pascoe. Don't stay up here counting rocks." "Goodnight, Emory." She walked the stacks a last time in the long light with the far rim gone gold and the near one already grey. The sound stack she counted by touch as she went, palm flat to each shoulder, eleven down to one. She dragged the tarp over the fresh course and set a waste block on each corner. The water bucket went out over the platform edge, and she heard it strike bottom in the gorge. Then there was nobody in the yard but her and the chest under its canvas. She threw the tarp back and opened the lid. Her father's chisel came out of its bed and went with her to the plank bench at the yard's edge. The ferrule came off with a drift and two taps. It went down on the plank beside the mallet. The wedge in the head sat proud of the ash, and she turned the tool over and knocked it through from the far side, and it fell and rolled until her thumb stopped it. Then she had the handle loose in her fingers. Elias Pascoe had worn a hollow into the ash below the ferrule where his forefinger rode it, and she moved her own finger up and down the shaft until it dropped into the hollow, and it sat wrong there, riding high on the second joint. Fourteen months of driving this chisel and her hand had never once gone to that place on its own. The oiled rag lay folded in the corner of the chest where he had always kept it, and the smell came up off the flannel when she reached past the tin. Boiled linseed and iron. He had oiled the heads on Sunday nights at the kitchen table in Wheeling, with the newspaper spread under them and her mother telling him to take the whole business out to the shed. She fitted the halves of the split together to see whether the handle would come back true. The ash closed along the grain and stood open at the ferrule end. She held it shut between her thumb and forefinger and turned it into what light was left on the plank. Then her hands stopped, and she sat down on the block behind her and put the chisel in her lap. The light went off the far rim and left the gorge in shadow up to the top course of the abutment. She sat with her hands open on either side of the tool and did not do anything with them. "Elias," she said. The gorge took the sound of it away downstream. She stayed on the block with the split handle lying across her thighs, her shoulders down and her breath coming shallow and high, and she let it come. Nobody stood on the rim road above the yard to see it. After a time she reached into the chest for the oiled rag. She wrapped the head and the split together and laid the chisel back in its bed, coarse to fine, heads to the hinge, and put the ferrule and the wedge in the small tin at the corner where he had always kept the loose parts. The lid came down. The tarp came over it and a waste block went on the windward corner to hold the canvas tight. Tomorrow's block stood at the head of the sound stack with the water mark still faint on its face. She took the keel out of her apron, laid her palm flat on the shoulder to steady the stone, and drew the line where the first cut would go. Eight inches along she drew a second mark for the joint. Then she stepped back and read the two lines together against the springer above them, and she went in and moved the second one a half inch to the left. She put her boot to the skid and rocked the block once to be certain it would not shift under a chisel in the morning. The crew would hear nothing about it, and neither would Sabra. The four days went into the schedule where the days went, buried under the fill courses with the rest of the week. "All right," she said to the stone. She hung the keel back in her apron and touched her own chisel through her belt. Then she went up the path to the rim road and walked it as far as the turnout above the yard, reading the wheel tracks and the sight lines down onto the stacks. When the next wagons came up that grade she would be standing in the yard ahead of them, to see who drove and who signed.
