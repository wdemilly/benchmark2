STEP 2A — ASSESS THREE DRAFTS AND SELECT ONE
(v1 — 2026-07-18. New step. Purpose: the same chapter is drafted three times by Step 2; this step reads all three as literature and selects the one to keep. This is a craft judgment, not a measurement. Derived from a close comparison of three Keystone chapter 2 drafts whose quality differences were large and identifiable; the criteria below are the differences that mattered there. To be run in a fresh incognito window with no other context. Amended 21 July 2026 on Walter's instruction: criteria 3 and 4 are swapped, so dialogue under load is now judged third and information strategy fourth. Reason: on Keystone chapter 10b the old order selected the draft that scored 84 over the draft that scored 91, and the assessment itself named the cause — it ranked the winning draft best on dialogue and then said the criterion order broke the tie against it. Under the new order it selects the 91.)

You are a fiction editor reading three drafts of the same chapter. All three were written from the same outline. Your job is to judge them as prose fiction — as an acquiring editor would — and select the strongest. You are not checking them against the outline for coverage; assume all three cover it.

Read all three drafts completely before writing anything.

Then assess each draft against these criteria, in this order. For every judgment you make, quote the exact passage it rests on. A judgment without a quotation does not count.

1. INTERIORITY. When the chapter goes inside the point-of-view character, is the interior material pinned to specific remembered places, objects, and people, or does it run to summary and abstraction? A memory with a name and a physical detail outranks a reflection that tells the reader what to conclude. Quote the best-pinned interior moment and the most abstract one in each draft.

2. THEME HANDLING. Does any character state the chapter's theme out loud? A speech that announces the meaning of the story is a defect, however well written. Behavior that carries the meaning without naming it is the standard. Quote the most thesis-like line in each draft and the best moment where meaning is carried by action alone.

3. DIALOGUE UNDER LOAD. Find the exchange that carries the most story weight in each draft. Does the dialogue do more than one job at a time — advance the plot, reveal the speaker, and obstruct the other character in the same lines? Quote the single best line of dialogue in each draft and say what jobs it is doing.

4. INFORMATION STRATEGY. What does each draft withhold, and how? The strongest choice lets the reader catch something a character misses, staged in plain sight without narration pointing at it. The weakest explains its own secrets. Identify each draft's central withheld fact and quote how it is planted.

5. MOTIF DISCIPLINE. Does a physical gesture or object recur with accumulated meaning — the same action returning later as metaphor, earned rather than installed? Quote the recurrence if it exists.

6. PRECISION OF THE PHYSICAL WORLD. Are measurements, procedures, and objects exact, consistent, and professionally credible throughout? Note any place where the physical facts blur, contradict, or go generic.

7. LINE FAULTS. Note clumsy constructions, repeated sentence shapes, tense wobbles, and dead phrases, with quotations. Weigh these last; a draft with the best architecture and a few rough lines beats a clean draft with weak architecture.

Then write the verdict:

- Rank the three drafts, first to third.
- For the winner, state in one paragraph why it wins, resting the paragraph on the quoted evidence above.
- For each loser, state in one or two sentences the decisive weakness.
- If two drafts are close, say so and say what would break the tie.
- Name the winner on its own final line, exactly: SELECTED: DRAFT [number].

Do not summarize the plot. Do not suggest revisions. Do not comment on the outline. Judge what is on the page.

----- DRAFT 1 -----

The ground had frozen again in the night. Nels Carrick set the pine post into the hole he and Philo Ruck had opened the evening before, held it upright with his knee against it, and waited while Philo brought the driver up from the wagon. "Give me an inch more on the north side," Nels said. Philo put his heel against the base and shifted it over. "That's your inch." Nels sighted down the run of set posts to the corner brace and back again. The line held. He took the driver, lifted it clear of the post top, and let it come down. The blow went flat into the cold and carried nowhere. He drove eleven times, checking the line at four and again at eight, and the post went a hand's width into ground that had given him nothing at all the previous spring. "She's in," Philo said. "Tamp it." Philo worked the spoil back around the base with the bar, kneeling on the frozen crust, packing it in lifts and stamping each one down. His hands were bare. Nels's gloves had worn through at the right forefinger in February. He had gone on wearing them. The knuckle under the hole had split in March, healed over, split again. Gray light was coming up over the Bighorns with no color anywhere in it. The grass ran thin from the fence line down toward the creek, April grass in a country that had not agreed to have a spring yet. Nels laid the driver in the wagon bed and counted what was left to set. Nine posts, and the swale to cross before dinner. Nels pulled his glove off and put his thumb along the post to check the plumb of it by feel. He had learned all of this at thirty-one years old from a Nebraska man in Cheyenne who was paid to teach him and never asked why a grown man wanted teaching. The swing of the driver still came a half second behind the thought of the swing. Nobody had ever remarked on it, and he had stopped expecting anyone to. "Ground's better past the swale," Philo said. "We'll see about that." They went on down the line. Nels dug, Philo cleared, and they traded off when Nels's shoulder went. By ten the nine posts stood and the corner brace was wired. Then they strung wire, working back from the brace. Philo unrolled the spool along the posts while Nels came behind with the fence pliers, taking up slack and setting staples where he had marked the wood in chalk. The strand rang when Philo put his weight on it. "Hold that." Philo held. Nels drove a staple over the taut wire, two blows, and left it a hair proud so the strand could travel in the heat that was coming. "Stock came through light," Philo said. "Lost the one heifer down by the water." "I saw her Tuesday." "Season's a month behind." "It is." Philo let the strand off and went for the next spool, and that was the whole of it. He was somewhere in his middle forties, leathered up to the eyes, Nebraska still sitting in his vowels after eleven years in the Territory. Five months he had drawn wages here. In that time he had never asked where Nels came from, what he had done before the ranch, or how a man arrived in this basin at thirty-two with cash for a quarter-section, forty head, and a hired hand. With the pliers still in his hand Nels settled it over again. He had hired Philo Ruck for the incuriosity, and the good hands were the smaller half of the bargain. "Wire's short," Philo said. "Two spools, maybe three." "I'm riding in to Keppler's after dinner." "Get the twelve-gauge if he's got it. The other stretches out on you by August." "He'll have it." Philo went down the line for the spool. Nels bent the loose end of the strand back on itself, twisted it off with the pliers, and set the cut piece in his coat pocket rather than leave it in the grass where a horse could find it. When the stretch was tied off Nels walked it with his thumb riding the top strand, testing every span between posts. The ridge stood north behind the house. The creek came down out of it and crossed the road a quarter mile above his gate, and the road ran on southeast to Roan Creek, four miles. Two approaches to the gate, then. There was the road, and there was the low ground along the water where a rider could come up out of sight until he was inside three hundred yards. Nels thumbed the last span, found it slack, and chalked the post for the afternoon. The house was one room and a lean-to. A stove, a table, two chairs, a bed frame he had built himself, and a shelf holding the things a man who ranched would own. There was a tin of matches, a Bible he had never opened past the flyleaf, a brand book from the county. He came in at eleven with the cold still in his coat and set the Kansas City feed catalogue on the table where it had been lying eight days. He thumbed it front to back, harness and seed prices and windmill parts going by in a blur, and stopped at page forty. The folded sheet lay flat in the gutter of the binding. Nels turned the cover over and read the shipping label along its edge before he touched the sheet. Burleigh never used the post office and never put a name on anything. The catalogue came addressed to the ranch from a supply house that existed and did business, mailed by a clerk who drew a federal stipend and never knew what he was mailing or to whom. Four had come in since December, and Nels had burned every one once he had the sheet out of it. Nels drew the sheet out and squared it in front of him. He turned the chair so his back was to the wall and the window sat on his left, the Bighorns standing in it gray to the shoulder with old snow. Then he got the pencil down off the shelf. The point had gone blunt. He shaved it once with his knife and turned a feed bill from March face down for a working sheet. The cipher was a substitution Burleigh had built off a page of the Denver city directory, and running it was a chore. Nels copied the coded line, ran the letters, struck through what came up wrong, and filled the plain text in above. VAN DYNE, he wrote. Then TWO MORE PARCELS. He struck a G that should have been a C and went on. The parcels had gone across in February through two companies registered in Denver, both of them a name on a door and a clerk paid by the month. He printed the company names out in full and sat looking at them with the pencil crosswise in his fingers. The next line took him twice through. SOMEONE IN ROAN CREEK. He worked it again to be certain, and the certainty put him back over the table. A local man was filing homestead claims for the syndicate, claims sworn to and witnessed and never lived on an hour. Every one of them ran the title into Van Dyne's hands inside sixty days. Eleven quarter-sections clouded that the Cheyenne office knew about. The General Land Office would not move on any of it without names and documents in hand. Burleigh put that last, and Nels filled the letters in above it one at a time: NAMES AND PAPER. NOTHING ELSE MOVES THEM. Nels set the pencil down and pushed back from the table. Five months in the basin had bought him the syndicate, the method, and eleven clouded titles. Nowhere on the sheet in front of him was the name of the man who swore the affidavits. He lifted the stove lid with the lifter and fed the letter in, then the feed bill with the plain text on it. The paper caught at a corner, stood up in the draft, and went over. He watched it down to ash, dropped the lid, and took his hat off the nail. The feed bin stood inside the shed door, oats in the top and the false bottom underneath, boards he had fitted himself so the seams ran with the grain of the sides. Nels scooped feed off into a bucket until the boards showed, worked two fingers into the notch, and lifted. The journal lay in the dry space below in an oilcloth wrap. He wrote standing, the book open on the lid of the bin, grain dust settling into the page. The pages ran back to the first week of December in the same hand, no name of his own anywhere in it, no name of Burleigh's, nothing a stranger could read and place. April 24. Two parcels Feb, Denver companies, names entered p. 14. Eleven titles clouded; local filer unidentified; Keppler, no proof. He turned back and read the Keppler entries, four of them now, the first from January. A merchant with the largest building in town. Credit out to half the basin, and the paper on it held in his own office rather than the bank's. Two trips to Denver over the winter with nothing coming back into the store to show for either one. Nothing in the four entries that a clerk in Cheyenne would read twice, and nothing Nels could carry to Burleigh and call evidence. He put the count at the foot of the page as he had every month since December. 5 mo. The journal went back into the oilcloth. Nels seated the boards and pressed along the seams with the heel of his hand until they closed flush, and there was no man between the Powder and the Big Horn who could stand up and say his name. He tipped the bucket and let the oats run back over the boards, worked the feed level, and took the saddle down off the rail. The road into Roan Creek followed the creek two miles before it climbed out of the bottom. Nels rode it at a walk with the cold coming down off the mountains behind him and passed the Halloran wagon going out, and lifted two fingers off the rein. The Halloran boy did the same and said something to his father that Nels did not catch. The town was one street. A bank, two saloons, the express office, the land office with its shade half drawn. The boarding house stood at the north end, and Keppler's Mercantile took up the middle of the block with a second story on it and a painted sign. Nels read the street coming in. Two wagons at the bank, a stack of green lumber at the corner, four horses along the rail in front of the near saloon and none of them strange to him. He tied at the hitch rail out front of the mercantile, looped the reins twice, and ran his hand down the horse's neck as he came around the head. Inside, the store smelled of coffee and coal oil. Nels laid his list on the counter, and the clerk, a young fellow named Dorsey in sleeve garters with ink on two fingers, read it upside down and started pulling stock. "Two spools of the twelve-gauge?" "Two." "Coffee's up a nickel since March." "Give me the five pounds regardless." Dorsey weighed the beans out and folded the paper down over them. He went to the keg for staples and counted nails into a scoop. Nels stood with his hat on and watched the street through the front glass. A man was walking a mule down the middle of it, taking his time about it. "Nails run four cents." "That's the lot, then." "You'll want the spools carried out?" "I'll manage them." Dorsey ran the column in the ledger and turned the book around so Nels could see the total. Nels counted coin out onto the wood, a dollar and eighty cents, and the clerk swept it off the edge into his palm without stopping to check it. "I could put this on account, Mr. Carrick. You're the only man in the county who won't carry one." "I'd hate to spoil your record." Dorsey laughed and wrote the entry and pushed the sack across the counter. The curtain at the back of the store went aside and Keppler came through it with his hat already in his hand. "Mr. Carrick." He came the length of the counter with the other hand out. "I was hoping you'd get in this week. How's that north fence coming?" "It's coming." Nels took the hand. Keppler held on to it a beat past the shake, his eyes going over Nels's face while he talked. "Hard ground this year. My man tells me the frost is still eighteen inches down in the shade of anything." Keppler wore a town coat of good wool and a collar that had been laundered somewhere other than Roan Creek. "You've done well out there. Everybody says so. Quiet outfit, no trouble with anybody, taxes paid before the county asks." "It suits me." "There's credit here for you whenever you want it. Any figure within reason. A man improving a place oughtn't to be paying cash across a counter for wire." "I'll keep it in mind." Keppler let go of his hand and turned to the man waiting at the end of the counter, a narrow fellow in a duster with a leather case held under his arm. Nels bent and got his shoulder under the sack, straightened, and settled the weight while the two of them started in. "...the Bidwell quarter comes open in June, and Ferris says the affidavit's already been—" The office door closed on the rest of it. Nels shifted the sack, took his change up off the counter, and went out. Narrow build, thirty-five or forty, a leather case with a strap worn white at the buckle. The duster hem was rubbed on the right side from a saddle. Ferris. He carried the name out onto the street with the sack on his shoulder. The stage had come in while he was inside. It stood empty in front of the express office with the team already led off, and a woman was crossing the street toward the boarding house with a carpetbag in her hand, a boy following her with a trunk on a barrow. She had come a long way and it was on her. She crossed at an even pace, and she went up the two steps of the porch without shifting the bag to her other hand. Nels lashed the sack behind his cantle, untied the reins from the rail, and mounted. He turned the horse south, and he had the Bidwell quarter and the name Ferris to work on for the four miles home. The light was long and yellow off the ridge when he came up his own road. He unsaddled in the shed and rubbed the horse down, forked hay into the manger, then carried the sack in and set the coffee on the shelf. He ate at the table with the lamp lit, beans and bacon and the end of Sunday's bread, on the one plate he owned. The second chair stood across from him where it had stood since he bought the pair of them in Cheyenne with the rest of the outfit. He ate everything and washed the plate in the basin and set it up on edge to drain. Then he went out and brought the journal in and opened it under the lamp. Ferris: name off Keppler's back counter. Bidwell quarter opens June. Affidavit prepared ahead of the filing. Under that he wrote: Land office. Every entry Keppler has witnessed, three years back. Copy the names. He sat with the pencil crosswise in his fingers and read the two entries. In June somebody would swear to a residence he had never kept a night in, and a clerk in this town would copy the oath into a book that any citizen could ask to see. Nels had spent five months in the basin asking for nothing. He wrote the date beside the entry and closed the book. He wrapped it in the oilcloth and carried it out to the shed, seated it under the boards, and poured the feed back over until the grain lay a hand deep across them. "Ferris," he said to the horse. Then he came out of the shed, latched the door behind him, and started up to the house working out what a man could say at that counter to get three years of Keppler's witness marks in front of him without giving anybody cause to remember the asking.


----- DRAFT 2 -----

Frost had gone back into the ground overnight, and the first swing of the post driver came off the top of the pine post with a sound that carried down to the creek. Nels lifted the driver again and set his feet. Philo Ruck stood on the far side of the hole with both gloved hands on the post, holding it plumb, his head turned a little away from the blow. "Give me another inch," Nels said. Philo leaned the top of the post a fraction east. Nels sighted down the four posts already set, back toward the corner brace, and found the new one true with them. He brought the driver down. The post went a hand's width and stopped. He worked the driver up and brought it down twice more, and the third blow took the post to the mark he had scratched on the wood the night before. "Hold it there." Philo went down on one knee, swept the loose dirt back off the lip of the hole with his glove, and took hold of the post again. The driver came down twice more and the post stopped moving under the blows. He set the driver against his leg, took up the shovel, and filled around the post in three lifts, tamping each one with the handle end until the ground rang dull instead of soft. His gloves had worn through at the right forefinger sometime in March and he had not gotten around to the other pair. Cold came off the Bighorns in the early light and lay in the low ground along the creek, white over grass that had no business still being that thin in the last week of April. "That'll hold anything short of a bull," Philo said. "It'll hold a bull." Philo took his hands off the post. It stood where they had put it, and he stepped back and looked at it with his head tipped, and then he went for the wire. Nels sighted the line one more time. Two years ago he could not have set a post that would stand through a season. He had learned it on purpose, the same as he had learned to shoe a horse and read a brand and talk about hay, from a man he paid by the week in a rented pasture outside Cheyenne, where nobody had thought it strange that a grown man wanted teaching. They strung wire down the new posts after that, Philo walking the coil out and Nels following with the stretcher. The strand came up off the ground and sang when it took tension. Philo held it flat against the wood while Nels drove the staple, two blows and a third to seat it, and the wire lay hard along the post. "Grass is three weeks behind," Philo said. "Stock's showing it." "It'll come." "Not till the nights let go." Philo dug in his coat pocket and had the staples out before Nels asked for them. "Fellow I worked for at Sheridan lost forty head in a spring like this one. Wouldn't feed past March. Said the calendar told him not to." "Cattle can't read." Philo made a sound that was almost a laugh and pulled the next stretch of wire up. He was somewhere in his mid-forties, with a face gone to leather above the white band at his collar line and Nebraska still sitting in his vowels. He had come off an outfit near Sheridan in November and he had never said what took him off it. Nels took the fence pliers back from him and trimmed the strand end. He had hired Philo out of a line of four men standing in the cold outside the livery. The other three had wanted to know where he was from. Philo had wanted the wage, and had asked about the bunk and the beef and nothing else, then or since. A man who took an interest would have taken an interest in everything. "Next one," Nels said, and picked up the driver. By mid-morning they had eleven posts in and better than two hundred yards of new wire on them. Nels walked the finished stretch alone, putting his thumb under each strand and lifting it an inch to feel the give. The ridgeline ran a mile north with the road cut along the base of it. Below him the creek went east through willow scrub, and the county road came in from the southwest and crossed his line at the gate. Anyone riding to the house came by that road or came down off the ridge into the creek bottom, and from the kitchen window a man could watch the gate and about half the ridge trail without getting up from the table. He thumbed the last strand, found it slack, and went back down the line for the stretcher. The catalogue had come up from the box at the gate two days before, a spring feed and implement list out of Omaha with a shorthorn bull on the cover. Nels set it on the kitchen table when he came in at noon and put the pot back on the stove. He turned pages with his thumb while he waited on the coffee, past the harrows, past four pages of seed, and at the sheet-metal stock tanks a folded sheet came up under his thumb. Burleigh never used the post office at Roan Creek and never put a name on anything he sent. The catalogue came from a jobber in Omaha who had no idea he was carrying federal mail. Nels put his thumb on the folded edge and left it there while the pot came up behind him. Then he drew the sheet out, squared it in front of him, and moved the coffee to the side of the table. There was a bed in the corner that he made every morning, a stove, two chairs, and the window with the mountains and the gate in it. He had put nothing on the walls in eleven months. The table took a saddle blanket over it when a neighbor came up the road, and the neighbors had come up the road four times since November, and every one of them had sat where the letter was lying now. He took a pencil off the shelf and the back of a feed bill down from the nail by the door, sharpened the pencil once with his knife, and started at the top line. The cipher was a running substitution off a keyword they changed with the month. April's was the name of a town in Missouri that neither of them had ever been to. Nels ran the keyword down the margin first, letter under letter, then worked across the coded line, and he did it at the pace of a man footing up a tally sheet. He wrote the plain letters above the coded ones and struck each coded letter through as he took it, and the first line came clear inside a minute. Van Dyne had taken two more parcels on the Powder River side. Nels copied the section and township numbers into the margin of the feed bill as they came. Both parcels sat on water. The pencil moved along and gave up the buyers, two companies chartered in Denver inside the last sixty days, both of them named for streets, neither with an officer either of them could put a face to. Nels wrote out the third line and it said the claims had gone through the land office at Roan Creek with the filing fees paid in cash by a resident of the county. It said the resident's name was on nothing. The fourth line put the count of clouded titles at nine. He read that one twice to be sure of the figure. Nine was three more than the count he had carried up from Cheyenne in November, and six of those had been filed inside this county. Burleigh's last line ran longer than the rest. The General Land Office would not move against Van Dyne on inference; the deputy commissioner wanted names, and he wanted the documents to hang them on, and until both came east the syndicate could go on buying the territory a quarter section at a time. He had the sheet done in ten minutes. Nels read the whole of it through once. Then he went back and read the third line again, and sat with the pencil on the table under his finger. The stove had come up hot. He lifted the lid with the lifter, fed the letter and the feed bill in together, and stood over it until the coded sheet went and the plain one after it. He set the lid back and knocked the ash down through the grate, and he stirred the ash with the lifter until nothing in it held a shape. The feed bin stood in the shed against the north wall. Nels scooped the oats down about a foot, worked his fingers into the notch cut in the end board, and lifted the false bottom out. The journal lay in the shallow space under it in oilcloth. He unwrapped it, opened it flat on the bin's lid, and wet the pencil. Feed dust had worked into the folds of the oilcloth and he brushed it off against his coat before he laid it down. He entered the two parcels first, then the two Denver companies with their street names under them. Below that he wrote: fees paid cash, resident, no name on file. Then the standing list. Nine titles clouded. Keppler, no evidence, five months running. He put the date at the head of the entry and went back through the pages to the first one, in November, and counted, and wrote the figure at the foot of the page. Five months. Grain dust turned in the light from the shed door. Nels blew across the page, shut the book, and wrapped the oilcloth back around it. The boards went down over it and he poured the oats back until the level came up to the ring the feed had worn in the wood. There was no one in the territory who could put his true name to him, and no one to give it to. He pressed the feed flat with the scoop, took the saddle down off the rail, and went out to catch the horse. Roan Creek lay four miles down the county road in a bend of the creek it was named for, one street of frame buildings with the mountains standing up behind them. Nels rode in a little after two with the cold still in the air and the dust coming up off the road regardless. He counted what was on the street as he came, out of habit: the freight wagon down at the depot end, three horses at the saloon rail, a boy sweeping the walk in front of the barber shop. Keppler's mercantile held the corner and half the block behind it, two stories of it, with a painted sign that had been repainted since fall. He tied up at the rail out front and looped the reins twice, and a man he had bought hay from in February raised a hand to him from across the street. Inside, the clerk had his ledger open on the counter and a pencil behind his ear. Nels laid his list down on the wood. "Staples, two pound. Roll of wire. Coffee. Ten pound of the eight-penny nails." "We've got the eight-penny in." The clerk went down the counter, came back with the coffee, and set it on a square of paper. "You want the four-penny while you're in? They came in Tuesday." "Next time." "Wire's out back. I'll have Amos carry it round to your horse." Nels watched him weigh the staples off and run the nails into the scale pan out of the keg, a scoop and a half and then a few off the top with his fingers to bring the needle down. The clerk wrote the four items into the ledger and totaled them. "Two dollars and sixty." Nels counted it out in coin on the counter. The clerk put the money in the drawer and entered the sale under Carrick without looking up to check the name, and he had not looked up when Nels came through the door either. "Anything else, Mr. Carrick?" "Not unless you've started selling rain." "I'd be a rich man." The clerk pushed the sack across. "Everybody in the county's asked me that this week." The curtain at the back of the store came aside and Keppler came through it with another man behind him. Keppler had on a town suit of gray broadcloth with a watch chain across it, brushed, and he came down the length of the counter with his hand already out. "Mr. Carrick. I was hoping I'd catch you in." Nels shook it. Keppler's grip was warm and dry, and he held on a beat past the end of the shake with his other hand coming up to rest on Nels's forearm. "How's the north country using you?" "Cold. Grass is behind." "It's behind everywhere. I've told Ellis to bring in more of the cottonseed cake this year, and I'll carry you on it if you want to take some now and settle after you sell in the fall. You've got the name for it in this store." "I pay as I go." "So I'm told." Keppler laughed and let go of the arm. "Well. If you change your mind, tell Ellis and he'll write it up. And if you ever get tired of running cows on that quarter of yours, come see me before you talk to anybody else. Ground up your way is worth more than what's standing on it." "It's doing what I want it to do." Keppler turned back to the other man, who had stayed at the end of the counter with his hat still on. Nels had not seen him in the county. He was young, thirty at the outside, and his coat was town-cut and had not been through a winter yet. Nels shifted the sack up onto his shoulder and settled it, and as he came past them the young man was saying, "—not before the survey clears, and then he'll want it put in his own—" Keppler said something across him about the weather, and that was all of it. Nels filed the coat and the face and the half of a sentence, and went out the door. The stage had come in while he was inside. It stood at the depot end of the street with the team already out of the traces, and a woman was coming up from it toward the boarding house with a carpetbag in her hand and a man behind her carrying a trunk on his shoulder. She had two days of train and a day of stage on her, and she kept an even pace up the walk with her chin level. Nels tied the sack on behind his saddle. He unlooped the reins, swung up, and turned the horse north out of the street, back to the third line of the letter and the man behind it, a resident of the county paying filing fees in cash with his name on nothing. The light had gone long and yellow off the ridge by the time he came up to his own gate. He led the horse through, shut it behind him, and at the corral he pulled the saddle, rubbed the animal down, and forked hay over the rail to it. In the house he lit the lamp and cut bacon into the pan and ate at the table, one plate in front of him and the other chair pushed in where it had been that morning. The last of the light went off the mountains while he ate. He put the plate in the wash pan and stood a minute with his hands on the back of the chair. Then he took the lamp out to the shed, dug down through the oats, and brought the journal back to the table. He wrote across the top of a clean page: Keppler, filings. Under it he set down what could be done inside a week. The tract books at the Roan Creek land office were public, and a rancher asking after the ground next to his own had a reason to ask. That put him in the office with the register open in front of him. Cash left a receipt, and a receipt carried the hand of the man who signed for it. Nels wrote: compare hands. Below that: every entry adjoining the two parcels, with dates. He read it over, closed the book, and carried it out to the shed. The oilcloth went around it, the boards went down over that, and he poured the feed back and pressed it level with the scoop. Then he took the lamp off the nail and went out to the corral to bring the horse up into the near pen, so it would be at the gate at first light. "Early," he told it, and put his hand on its neck and walked it in.


----- DRAFT 3 -----

The hole was down thirty inches and the last four had come out of the ground in chips. Nels Carrick set the post in it, turned it until the flat face lay along the line, and put his shoulder to it while he sighted down the run toward the creek. "East a hair," he said. Philo Ruck moved the top of the post with two fingers and held it there. "More." "That's a hair and a half." "Then it's right." "Ground's froze at the bottom of that hole yet," Philo said. "It'll take." Nels took up the driver, a capped length of pipe with handles welded to it, and lifted it over the post. The first blow went into the ground without ringing. He drove eleven times. After the fourth stroke and again after the eighth he walked two steps out to check the line against the posts already set, then came back and set his feet before he swung. The chips came up around the base on the fourth stroke. On the seventh the post stopped moving altogether and after that he was only seating it. He had learned this at thirty-two, in a bought month outside Laramie, from a hand who believed he was teaching an easterner with money and a notion. The swing still came a half second behind the thought. That half second was in every stroke, and the post went down plumb. The glove on his right hand was worn through at the first finger. He had been meaning since February to trade it out, and the trade would mean a trip to Keppler's, and the trip to Keppler's had other uses, so the glove stayed. Cold light was on the Bighorns to the west, the flat white light of a spring three weeks late. Thin grass came up through last year's mat in patches the color of old rope. Philo let go of the post and toed dirt in around it. Nels tamped it with the bar until the sound changed from a knock to a dull thud, then hit it twice more and put his weight against the top with both hands. It gave nothing. He counted the run: nineteen posts set since Monday, forty-one to the corner. They strung wire along the new posts through the middle of the morning. Philo carried the staples loose in his coat pocket and passed the fence pliers over before Nels had put his hand out for them. He was somewhere in his mid-forties, leathered up to the hat line and white above it, with Nebraska still sitting in his vowels after nine years in the territory. "Bottom strand's slack," Philo said. "I'll get it." Nels drew the strand up, put his boot to the post, and held the tension while he hammered a staple over the wire. The staple went crooked and he pulled it, straightened it against the post, and drove it again. He had hired Philo in December out of four men who came for the place. Three of them had asked where he was from, and Philo had asked what the wage was and whether he ate at the house. Nels paid him a dollar and a half a day for that. "Grass is behind," Philo said. "Three weeks. Maybe four." "It'll come." "It came late in eighty-one and the stock got through it. Then it came late in eighty-six." Philo spat off to the side of the line. "You'll want to be running less than you're carrying by August." "I'll see what the hay does." "Sweeney lost a heifer Tuesday. Cold, they're saying." "I heard it was the cold." "Man's got no windbreak on that draw." Nels drew the next span up and held it while Philo set the staple. They worked down four posts that way, one holding and one hammering, trading without a word said about the trade. Philo cut the wire at the corner, made the twist without looking up at him, and put the pliers back in Nels's hand. "That'll hold cattle," he said. "It won't hold that bay of yours if he decides on the oats." "Then I'll keep the oats shut." At noon Nels walked the finished stretch alone with his thumb riding the top wire, testing each span between posts. The ridge stood north of him with the creek bottom under it. The road came in from the east and split half a mile out, one fork to his gate, the other running past the corner of his fence toward Sweeney's. Two approaches, then, and the second one visible for four hundred yards from the kitchen window. He put his thumb under the last strand, pushed up, and felt it give more than he wanted. The house was two rooms and a stove. He had bought the furniture in one afternoon and had added nothing to it in five months. A stranger would find a table, a bed, a shelf of tinned goods, and a feed catalogue from a Cheyenne supply house that came four times a year. The catalogue was on the porch bench where Philo had left it with the rest of the mail, four ounces of harrow teeth and windmill parts and a page of prices for cottonseed cake. Nels brought it in, set it square on the kitchen table, and thumbed it from the back with his thumb riding the block of pages. Burleigh had never once used the post office at Roan Creek and had never once written the name Carrick. The catalogue came from a Cheyenne supply house that mailed to every stockman in six counties, and it came whether Nels ordered from it or not. His thumb stopped between pages forty and forty-one. He drew out a folded sheet the size of his hand and let the catalogue fall shut. He sat down with the window at his left, the Bighorns standing in the top half of it, and squared the sheet in front of him with two fingers. The coded line ran in blocks of five. He took the stub of pencil off the shelf, opened his knife, and put a point on it with four strokes. Then he turned over a feed bill from March and began to work the substitution across the top of the blank side, writing each plain letter above its coded letter and striking the coded letter through as he took it. VAN DYNE, he wrote, and struck the block. TWO MORE PARCELS. The pencil went along the line. NORTH OF THE CREEK ROAD, and then the two names the parcels had been taken in, both of them companies registered in Denver in January, neither of them owning so much as a wagon. He had read the first name on a deed in Cheyenne in the fall, on land two hundred miles south of here. He wrote it again in the margin and put a box around it. The pencil point flattened and he turned it a quarter turn against the paper and kept on. FILINGS MADE LOCALLY. He worked the next block twice to be sure of it. SOMEONE AT ROAN CREEK IS SIGNING HOMESTEAD CLAIMS FOR THEM. FOURTEEN SINCE OCTOBER. The claimants were men who had never broken sod on the parcels they swore to. Three of the fourteen had signed with a mark. The titles behind them were clouded four deep, and every one of the fourteen would stand up in a territorial court until somebody produced the man who prepared the papers. BURLEIGH ADDS: PROOF WITNESSES REPEAT. SAME TWO NAMES ON SIX. Nels wrote that above the block and went back over it letter by letter, and it came out the same. TITLES CLOUDED. GLO WILL NOT MOVE WITHOUT NAMES AND DOCUMENTS. He wrote the last block, struck it, and set the pencil down. Ten minutes, and the work of it was the work of adding a column of figures. Fourteen filings, and he could name none of the men, and the office would sit on its hands until he could. He read the plain text through once more from the top and pressed the pencil dust off the page with the side of his hand. He lifted the stove lid with the lifter, laid the letter on the coals with the feed bill folded over it, and watched the pencil work go brown and then black at the edges before it took. The paper stood up in the draft, caught along one corner, and dropped. When the flame fell he pushed the ash apart with the lifter, turned the black sheets under, and set the lid back down. The feed bin stood against the north wall of the shed with a hundred pounds of oats in it. He scooped the oats to one end with the coffee tin he kept for it, pried up the board at the back corner with his knife, and lifted the false bottom out and against his knee. The journal was underneath in oilcloth. He unwrapped it, opened it on the raised lid of the bin, and licked the pencil and wrote the date. Two parcels, north of the creek road. Denver names, both. Fourteen filings since October, local hand. Same two witnesses on six. Titles clouded. He wrote the January deed name in and drew a line from it to the parcel it had turned up on in the fall. Keppler, still nothing. He wrote Keppler's name with a question stroke after it, as he had written it in February and again in March, and he had put no document beside it either time. Under that he wrote the running count of the days: one hundred and fifty-one. Grain dust came up off the oats onto the open page. He blew it clear and closed the book. He wrapped the oilcloth, laid the journal flat, and seated the false bottom over it, pressing the boards down at all four corners until they sat level. Then he scooped the oats back across them by the double handful until the surface sat level with the rest. In the territory of Wyoming there was not one living person who could say his name. He hauled the last of the grain over the boards, went out, and took the saddle down off the rail. The four miles into Roan Creek took him forty minutes at a walk, the horse blowing steam the whole way. He came in past the livery with the wind carrying grit off the street. Keppler's store stood two storeys and painted at the middle of the block, with the bank and the hotel low on either side of it. Nels put his horse at the rail outside and looped the reins twice. Two freighters were unloading at the far end and a boy was holding their leaders. A woman he knew by sight went into the milliner's. The Mabry boy raised a hand to him from a doorway. Nels raised one back and went in under the awning. Inside, the clerk had the ledger open before Nels reached the counter. "Mr. Carrick." "Ten pound of staples. Two spools of wire, the heavy. Coffee, five pound. And nails." "How many nails?" "Fifteen pound." The clerk went down the keg with the scoop and set the weights on the pan a handful at a time. "Sixteen and a quarter. I'll call it fifteen." He tipped the nails into the sack and folded the top. "You buy nails like a man putting up a town." "One gate," Nels said. "Four dollars sixty." "Put the coffee in separate." "It's in the sack there." The clerk pulled it out and set it on the wood by itself. Nels counted the coin out onto the counter. The clerk swept it off the edge into his hand without counting it after him, wrote the sum in the ledger, and turned the page over on it. Five months of paying cash on the day, saying what he came for, and going out again, had bought him a man who did not look up when he came in. Keppler came through from the back room while the clerk was tying the sack. "Carrick." He put his hand out over the counter and Nels took it. "I've been meaning to catch you. That north fence of yours." "Going up this week." "Good country up there. Cold, but good." Keppler held the handshake a beat past its use, and let it go. He was in a gray suit cut in Chicago or better, and his collar was clean at four in the afternoon on a street carrying three inches of dust. "You're paying cash for everything you carry out of here. I'd carry you on the books to fall, and gladly. Any man running stock through a spring like this one." "I'll keep on as I am." "That's your business, and it's a rare enough business in this town." Keppler laughed at that, a short pleasant laugh, and rested both hands on the counter. "Delivery, then. I've a wagon going out past Sweeney's Thursday and it's no trouble to set your wire off at your gate." "I'll carry it." "Suit yourself." Keppler smiled at him, pleased with him, and turned back toward the stove where a man in a brown coat was standing with his hat still on. Nels had not put eyes on him before, in this town or another. Fifty or near it, city boots under trail mud that had been brushed at, a leather case standing against his leg. Nels took the sack up onto his shoulder. Shifting it turned him halfway toward the stove, where the man in the brown coat was saying, "—both filed under the wife's name, so there's no reading it off the—" Keppler put a hand on the man's arm and moved him toward the back room, talking about the freight rates out of Buffalo the whole way. The door shut on the rest of it. Nels settled the sack across his shoulder, took the coffee under his other arm, and went out past the plow display and the awning post into the wind. Brown coat, fifty, city boots brushed at, leather case, the four-fifteen coach or a private rig from the north. He put it where he put things. The stagecoach had come in while he was inside. It stood at the far corner with the team already unhooked, and a woman was crossing toward the boarding house with a carpetbag in her right hand, a man behind her carrying a trunk on his shoulder. She walked at a pace she had chosen, slow and level, tired, carrying the tiredness where the street would not see it. Nels put the sack behind his saddle and lashed it down. She went up onto the boarding-house porch and out of the light. He unwound the reins from the rail, swung up, and turned the horse east toward the creek road. By the corner of the livery he was back on the man in the brown coat and the wife's name. The light was long and yellow across the flat by the time he came up his own road. He pulled the saddle, rubbed the horse down, and turned him into the corral with the last of the good hay. In the house he lit the lamp, cut bacon into the pan, and ate at the table off the single plate with the fork he had bought in Cheyenne. The other chair had gone for firewood in January. When he was finished he wiped the plate, set it on the shelf, and went back out to the shed with the lamp. He dug the oats away, lifted the boards, and took the journal up onto the lid of the bin again. Under the day's entries he wrote what he would do. Get at the filings themselves. Any claim Keppler's paper had touched would carry the same hand on it, and a hand could be matched, and the land office at Buffalo kept the originals where a man with a plausible reason could ask to see them. He wrote: BUFFALO LAND OFFICE, WEDNESDAY. ASK TO SEE THE ORIGINALS. Under it he added: WHO WITNESSES THEM. He wrapped the journal, laid it in, and pressed the false bottom down over it, corner by corner, until it sat flat. The oats went back over the boards and he leveled them with his forearm. The lamp threw his shadow up the shed wall and there was nothing else in the building to throw one. He set the boards of the bin lid down, turned the wick low, and stood a moment with his hand flat on the grain. Buffalo was thirty miles, and the bay had been favoring the near fore since Friday. Nels Carrick took the lantern off the nail and went out to the corral to get the foot up before he slept.
